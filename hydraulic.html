Physics

1 · The syringe picture

Put your thumb over the nozzle of a syringe full of water and press: the plunger hardly moves. In this model oil behaves the same way, because its density is fixed. Every cubic centimetre the input piston pushes out of the left cylinder has to reappear in the right one. If the input piston moves down by x₁ it sweeps out the volume A₁x₁, so the output piston rises by x₂ with

A₁·x₁ = A₂·x₂ ⇒ x₂ = r·x₁, r = A₁/A₂ = (d₁/d₂)²

With d₁ = 4 cm and d₂ = 12 cm, r = 1/9: the full 30 cm input stroke lifts the load by only 3.33 cm. The same rule fixes every speed in the oil. The left column moves at ẋ, the right column at r·ẋ, and the pipe at (A₁/Ap)·ẋ = 16·ẋ, because the pipe's cross-section is 16 times smaller than the input bore. That is why the dots race through the pipe.

2 · Pascal's principle

In oil at rest, the pressure is the same at all points at the same height. A pressure p pushes on a piston of area A with a force p·A. Push the small piston with F₁ and the oil carries p = F₁/A₁ to the large piston, which feels

F₂ = p·A₂ = F₁·A₂/A₁

With the defaults this gives 170 N × 9 = 1530 N. The load and its piston weigh (m₂+M)·g = 160 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 1569.6 N, so 170 N on its own would not lift them. The 2 kg input piston tips the balance: its weight of 19.6 N also presses on the oil, and the threshold is F₁,min = (m₂+M)·g·r − m₁·g = 154.78 N. Height matters too. In still oil, pressure rises by ρg = 8.53 kPa per metre of depth, and once the press moves the two columns are no longer equally tall.

3 · Energy: what you gain in force you pay in distance

The press multiplies force by 9 and divides distance by 9, so the product does not change, just as with a lever:

F₁·x₁ = p·A₁·x₁ = p·A₂·x₂ = F₂·x₂

In the moving press, the work you put in, W = ∫F₁ dx, goes to three places:

  • the kinetic energy K = ½μẋ² of everything that moves;
  • the potential energy V(x): the load is lifted, the input piston is lowered, and oil is moved from the top of the left column to the top of the right one;
  • the energy lost when a stop halts a moving piston instantly.

The page checks W = K + V + Elost at every recorded instant.

4 · One coordinate, one Lagrangian

Volume conservation leaves a single degree of freedom: x, the downward displacement of the input piston. The column heights are h₁ = H₀ − x and h₂ = H₀ + r·x. Adding the kinetic energies of the parts, with plug flow:

T = ½m₁ẋ² + ½(m₂+M)(rẋ)² + ½ρA₁h₁ẋ² + ½ρApLp(A₁ẋ/Ap)² + ½ρA₂h₂(rẋ)² = ½·μ(x)·ẋ² μ(x) = m₁ + (m₂+M)r² + ρA₁[(H₀ − x) + r(H₀ + r·x) + LpA₁/Ap]

The pipe term dominates. The pipe holds only 55 g of oil, but that oil moves 16 times faster than the piston, so its kinetic energy counts 16² = 256 times over. It contributes 14.0 kg of the 18.5 kg you feel through the piston.

V(x) = −m₁g·x + (m₂+M)g·r·x + ½ρgA₁(1+r)·x²

The last term has a simple picture. A slab of oil of volume A₁x leaves the top of the left column, where its centre was at H₀ − x/2, and is laid on top of the right column, where its centre is at H₀ + r·x/2. It rises by (1+r)·x/2, so it acts as a spring of stiffness k = ρgA₁(1+r) = 11.9 N/m. Lagrange's equation with the generalized force F₁(t):

d/dt(∂T/∂ẋ) − ∂T/∂x + ∂V/∂x = F₁(t) ⇒ μ(x)·ẍ + ½μ′·ẋ² = F₁(t) − V′(x), μ′ = dμ/dx = ρA₁(r² − 1)

As x grows, oil leaves the fast left column and joins the slow right column, so the moving mass shrinks (μ′ < 0 when r < 1). The ½μ′ẋ² term accounts for that exchange, like a chain sliding off the edge of a table. The page integrates this equation with fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4), a step of 10⁻⁴ s, and a recorded frame every 1 ms. The force ramps linearly from 0 to F₁ over τ.

5 · The pressure field inside the oil

Newton's law for each piston gives the pressure under it (a = ẍ):

m₁·a = F₁ + m₁g − p₁A₁ ⇒ p₁ = (F₁ + m₁g − m₁a)/A₁ (m₂+M)·r·a = p₂A₂ − (m₂+M)g ⇒ p₂ = (m₂+M)(g + r·a)/A₂

Now follow the oil from the input piston to the output piston, with v = ẋ and vp = (A₁/Ap)·v:

bottom of left column pb1 = p₁ + ρ(g − a)·h₁ weight of the column, less what its downward acceleration needs pipe inlet pin = pb1 + ½ρ(v² − vp²) Bernoulli: speeding the oil up costs pressure pipe outlet pout = pin − ρ(A₁/Ap)·a·Lp force that accelerates the oil plug in the pipe bottom of right column pb2 = pout + ½ρ(vp² − r²v²) slowing down gives the pressure back (lossless here) top of right column pb2 − ρ(g + r·a)·h₂ = p₂ must agree with the output piston

The last line is a self-test. The value at the end of the chain has to equal the p₂ that the output piston needs, and check_physics.py proves it symbolically. The oil is coloured by this pressure, pale amber at 0 and deep wine at the maximum of the run. Gauge pressure is pressure above the atmosphere. Below zero the oil is under suction, and a real press would draw in air or release dissolved gas there; near −100 kPa gauge the oil would boil (cavitate). The page warns as soon as any gauge pressure drops below 0.

6 · What a run does

  • rest: the load sits on its lower stop until F₁(t) > F₁,min. The oil is static: p₁ = (F₁+m₁g)/A₁ and p₂ = p₁ + ρg(h₁−h₂). The stop carries the rest of the load.
  • moving: the equation of motion is integrated.
  • end: the input piston hits its stop after a 30 cm stroke. Its velocity drops to 0 at once, the kinetic energy is recorded as lost, and the static pressures p₂ = (m₂+M)g/A₂ and p₁ = p₂ + ρg(h₂−h₁) are shown for 0.6 s.
  • If the load falls back to x = 0 with v < 0, the same inelastic stop applies and the press returns to rest.
  • stalled: if F₁ never exceeds F₁,min, the run ends 1 s after the ramp.
  • time limit: any other run ends at t = τ + 10 s. With a force that only ramps up, the energy function h = K + V − F₁(t)·x can only decrease, because dh/dt = −Ḟ₁·x ≤ 0. So the load can never come back to x = 0 with v < 0. If the force is too small to reach the stroke stop, the load swings between two turning points forever, and the run needs a limit.

7 · What “ideal” leaves out

  • The oil is incompressible (infinite bulk modulus), and the walls and pistons are rigid.
  • There is no viscosity: no friction loss along the pipe (Hagen–Poiseuille) and no damping.
  • Plug flow: one speed across each cross-section.
  • Area changes are lossless: Bernoulli holds at the contraction and at the expansion, with no Borda–Carnot loss where the jet leaves the pipe.
  • There is no friction at the piston seals and there are no leaks.
  • The turns from the columns into the pipe are ignored, and the pipe lies horizontal at the base of the columns.
  • The stops are perfectly rigid and perfectly inelastic: the velocity jumps to zero in zero time.
  • The oil carries no air, and all pressures are gauge pressures (atmosphere = 0). g = 9.81 m/s² everywhere.
