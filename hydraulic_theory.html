0 How to read this

This text derives, line by line, every equation that the two animations integrate. Nothing is quoted without being derived from Newton's laws, conservation of mass, or conservation of energy. You need single-variable calculus, a little vector calculus, and, for Part III, the Laplace transform and some linear algebra.

Each derivation is built the same way. A picture tells you what to imagine with your hands and eyes. The working is the mathematics, one step per line. A check tests the result against units and limiting cases. In our press puts in the numbers the animation uses, so you can compare with what you see on screen.

Conventions
  • SI units throughout. A pressure written p is a gauge pressure, measured above the atmosphere; the absolute pressure is pabs=p+p0 with p0=101.325 kPa.
  • A dot is a time derivative, x˙=dx/dt. A prime is a derivative with respect to a position coordinate, μ′=dμ/dx.
  • Flows Q are volume flows in m3/s; one litre per minute is 1.667×10−5 m3/s.
  • Numbers quoted “in our press” are for the default settings of the pages.
Part IThe ideal press: Pascal, Bernoulli, Lagrange

1 Pressure, and why it spreads

1.1 Pressure has no direction

Press your palm flat into a bathtub and then turn it sideways: the push of the water on your palm does not change with its orientation, only with how deep it is.

Take a tiny wedge of fluid at rest: a right-angled prism with a vertical face of height dy, a horizontal face of width dx, and a sloping face of length ds at angle θ, all of depth dz into the page. The geometry gives dx=dscosθ and dy=dssinθ. Call the pressures on the three faces px, py and pn. In a fluid at rest there is no shear, so every force is normal to its face.

Balance the horizontal forces. The vertical face pushes with pxdydz; the sloping face pushes back with the horizontal part of pndsdz:

pxdydz−pndsdzsinθ=0⇒pxdssinθdz=pndssinθdz⇒px=pn.(1.1)

Balance the vertical forces. Now the weight of the wedge, 12ρgdxdydz, also appears:

pydxdz−pndsdzcosθ−12ρgdxdydz=0⇒py−pn=12ρgdy.(1.2)

Shrink the wedge to a point: dy→0, so py=pn. The weight is a volume force (third order in size) and loses to the surface forces (second order). Since the angle θ was arbitrary, the pressure at a point is the same in every direction: it is a scalar.

1.2 Hydrostatics: pressure grows with depth

Cut a horizontal slab of area A and thickness dz out of the fluid, as in Figure 1. It does not move, so the three vertical forces cancel:

p(z)A−p(z+dz)A−ρgAdz=0.

Divide by Adz and let dz→0:

dpdz=−ρg⇒p(z)=p(z0)−ρg(z−z0).(1.3)
Check
Units: kg/m3·m/s2=N/m3=Pa/m, a pressure per metre. Sign: going up (z increasing) the pressure falls, as it should.
In our press
For oil of ρ=870 kg/m3, ρg=8.53 kPa per metre of depth. The 0.40 m columns of the ideal press add only 3.4 kPa at their base, against 150 kPa under the input piston.

1.3 Pascal's principle and the force multiplier

Equation (1.3) says that pressure differences in a fluid at rest depend only on height differences. Raise the pressure at one point by Δp, wait for the fluid to settle, and the pressure everywhere rises by the same Δp. That is Pascal's principle. Put a piston of area A1 on one side and a piston of area A2 on the other, at the same height:

p=F1A1=F2A2⇒F2=F1A2A1.(1.4)
In our press
d1=4 cm and d2=12 cm give A2/A1=9. A push of 170 N becomes 1530 N. The load and its piston weigh 160×9.81=1569.6 N, so the push alone would not lift them; the 19.6 N weight of the input piston tips the balance (section 4.7).

2 Continuity: oil cannot be lost

Put your thumb over the nozzle of a syringe full of water and push: the plunger hardly moves. Water, and nearly so oil, refuses to change volume. Whatever volume you push in somewhere must come out somewhere else.

Treat the oil as incompressible: its density ρ is a constant. Mass conservation then becomes volume conservation. When the input piston moves down by dx1 it sweeps the volume A1dx1; the only place for it is under the output piston, which must rise by dx2:

A1dx1=A2dx2⇒x2=rx1,r=A1A2=(d1d2)2.(2.1)

Divide by dt to get the speeds. The same rule fixes the speed everywhere in the oil if we assume plug flow, one speed across each cross-section. With v=x˙1:

A1v⏟left column=Apvp⏟pipe=A2(rv)⏟right column⇒vp=A1Apv.(2.2)
In our press
r=1/9: the full 30 cm stroke lifts the load by 3.33 cm. The 1 cm pipe has A1/Ap=16, so the oil in the pipe runs 16 times faster than the piston. That is why the tracer dots race through it.

3 Energy: the hydraulic lever

The work done by the input piston on the oil, as it moves by dx1 against pressure p, is dW1=F1dx1=pA1dx1=pdV. The work the oil does on the output piston is dW2=pA2dx2=pdV, with the same dV by (2.1). So

F1x1=F2x2.(3.1)

Force is multiplied by 9 and distance divided by 9. An ideal hydraulic press is a lever made of oil: it trades distance for force and never creates energy. In the moving press the input work goes three places: kinetic energy, potential energy, and the losses at the stops. The page checks that bookkeeping at every frame.

4 The moving press: one coordinate, one Lagrangian

4.1 One degree of freedom

Continuity ties every moving part to the input piston. Choose x, the downward displacement of the input piston, as the single generalized coordinate. Everything else follows from (2.1) and (2.2): the output piston rises by rx; the left column height is h1=H0−x; the right column height is h2=H0+rx.

4.2 Kinetic energy, part by part

Add up 12mv2 for every moving mass, using the speeds of (2.2). Each oil column of height h and area A has mass ρAh:

T=12m1x˙2input piston+12(m2+M)(rx˙)2output piston and load+12ρA1h1x˙2left column+12ρApLp(A1Apx˙)2oil in the pipe+12ρA2h2(rx˙)2right column(4.1)

Every term is proportional to x˙2, so we can factor it out. Use A2r2=A1r in the last term and Ap(A1/Ap)2=A1(A1/Ap) in the pipe term:

T=12μ(x)x˙2,μ(x)=m1+(m2+M)r2+ρA1[(H0−x)+r(H0+rx)+LpA1Ap].(4.2)

μ is the effective mass: the mass you would feel in your hand if you pushed the input piston.

In our press
The pipe holds only ρApLp=55 g of oil, but it moves 16 times faster than the piston, and kinetic energy goes with the square of speed. It counts 162=256 times: 14.0 kg of the total μ=18.5 kg. Narrow pipes make heavy machines.

4.3 Potential energy

Measure heights from the pipe. The input piston face sits at h1 and the output piston face at h2. An oil column of height h has its centre of mass at h/2, so its potential energy is ρAhgh/2. The oil in the horizontal pipe does not change height. Collect everything and subtract the value at x=0:

V(x)=m1gh1+(m2+M)gh2+12ρg(A1h12+A2h22)−V0.

Expand with h1=H0−x and h2=H0+rx. The pistons give −m1gx+(m2+M)grx. The columns give

12ρg[A1(x2−2H0x)+A2(r2x2+2H0rx)]=12ρg[A1x2+A1rx2]=12ρgA1(1+r)x2,

because A2r=A1 makes the linear terms cancel. Therefore

V(x)=−m1gx+(m2+M)grx+12ρgA1(1+r)x2.(4.3)
The last term has a simple picture: a slab of oil of volume A1x is taken from the top of the left column, where its centre was at H0−x/2, and laid on top of the right column, where its centre is at H0+rx/2. It rises by (1+r)x/2. The oil acts as a soft spring of stiffness k=ρgA1(1+r)=11.9 N/m.

4.4 The equation of motion

The applied force F1(t) is not derived from a potential, so it enters as a generalized force: in a virtual displacement δx it does work F1δx. Lagrange's equation with L=T−V reads

ddt∂T∂x˙−∂T∂x+∂V∂x=F1(t).(4.4)

Work out each piece. From (4.2), ∂T/∂x˙=μx˙. Its time derivative needs the chain rule, because μ depends on x and x on t:

ddt(μx˙)=μx¨+dμdxdxdtx˙=μx¨+μ′x˙2,∂T∂x=12μ′x˙2.

Insert into (4.4):

μ(x)x¨+12μ′x˙2=F1(t)−V′(x)μ′=ρA1(r2−1).(4.5)

This is the equation the first animation integrates, with fourth-order Runge–Kutta and a step of 10−4 s.

4.5 What the strange term means

The term 12μ′x˙2 appears because the effective mass changes as the press moves. As x grows, oil leaves the left column, where it moved at speed v, and joins the right column, where it moves at only rv. With r<1 the moving mass shrinks: μ′<0. Each bit of oil that changes columns gives up momentum, and the term accounts for that exchange. A chain sliding off the edge of a table obeys an equation of the same form.

4.6 When does the load start to move?

At rest, x=0 and x˙=0, (4.5) gives μx¨=F1−V′(0). The load leaves its stop only if this is positive:

F1>F1,min=V′(0)=(m2+M)gr−m1g.(4.6)
In our press
F1,min=160×9.819−2×9.81=174.40−19.62=154.78 N.

4.7 An energy function, and why the load never falls back

For a Lagrangian with a time-dependent force, define h=T+V−F1(t)x. Differentiate along a solution and use (4.5):

dhdt=μx˙x¨+12μ′x˙3+V′x˙−F˙1x−F1x˙=x˙(μx¨+12μ′x˙2+V′−F1)−F˙1x=−F˙1x.(4.7)

The ramp only increases (F˙1≥0) and x≥0, so h can only decrease. It starts at 0 when the load lifts off. At x=0 we have h=T≥0, which is only possible with T=0. The load can come back to its stop, at best, with zero speed: it never falls back onto it. If the force is too small to reach the stroke stop, the load swings forever between two turning points. That is why the page needs a time limit.

5 The pressure field inside a moving fluid

5.1 Newton's law for a fluid particle

Follow a small cylinder of fluid of cross-section dA and length ds along a streamline, with s measured along the flow. Its mass is ρdAds. Its acceleration is the rate of change of its speed v(s,t) as it moves, which has two parts: the speed changes in time at a fixed place, and the particle moves to a place where the speed is different:

DvDt=∂v∂t+v∂v∂s.

The forces along s are the pressure difference across its two ends, −∂p∂sdsdA, and the component of its weight, −ρgdAds∂z∂s. Newton's second law, divided by dAds:

ρ(∂v∂t+v∂v∂s)=−∂p∂s−ρg∂z∂s.(5.1)

This is Euler's equation along a streamline. Note v∂v/∂s=∂(12v2)/∂s. Integrate from point 1 to point 2:

p2−p1=−ρ∫12∂v∂tds−12ρ(v22−v12)−ρg(z2−z1).(5.2)

This is the unsteady Bernoulli equation. The first term is the push needed to accelerate the fluid between the two points, the second the push needed to speed it up, the third the weight.

5.2 Walking through the press

Apply (5.2) to each stretch of oil, with v=x˙, a=x¨ and vp=(A1/Ap)v.

  1. Down the left column, from the piston face to the bottom. The oil moves down at speed v and accelerates at a; going down, z falls by h1. With no speed change along the column, (5.2) gives
    pb1=p1+ρ(g−a)h1.(5.3)
  2. Into the pipe. Over a very short contraction the speed rises from v to vp; the time-derivative and height terms vanish as the length goes to zero:
    pin=pb1+12ρ(v2−vp2).(5.4)
  3. Along the pipe. The speed is the same everywhere, ∂vp/∂t=(A1/Ap)a, and the pipe is level:
    pout=pin−ρA1ApaLp.(5.5)
  4. Out of the pipe, slowing from vp to rv without loss:
    pb2=pout+12ρ(vp2−r2v2).(5.6)
  5. Up the right column, speed rv, acceleration ra, rising by h2:
    ptop=pb2−ρ(g+ra)h2.(5.7)

5.3 The field closes on both pistons

The pistons give two more equations. Newton for the input piston, pushed down by F1 and its weight, pushed up by p1A1: m1a=F1+m1g−p1A1. Newton for the output piston and load, rising with acceleration ra: (m2+M)ra=p2A2−(m2+M)g. So

p1=F1+m1g−m1aA1,p2=(m2+M)(g+ra)A2.(5.8)

Add (5.3) to (5.7). The Bernoulli terms combine to 12ρ(v2−r2v2) and the heights to −ρg(h2−h1)=−ρg(1+r)x:

p1−ptop=ρa(h1+rh2+LpA1Ap)+12ρ(r2−1)v2+ρg(1+r)x.(5.9)

Is this the same as what the pistons demand? Subtract the two equations (5.8), using p2A1=p2A2r:

(p1−p2)A1=F1+m1g−m1a−(m2+M)(g+ra)r.

Replace F1 with the equation of motion (4.5), F1=μa+12μ′v2+V′, where

V′=−m1g+(m2+M)gr+ρgA1(1+r)x.

The piston weights cancel, and so do the piston masses inside μ:

(p1−p2)A1=[μ−m1−(m2+M)r2]a+12μ′v2+ρgA1(1+r)x=ρA1(h1+rh2+LpA1Ap)a+12ρA1(r2−1)v2+ρgA1(1+r)x.

Divide by A1: this is exactly (5.9) with ptop=p2. The pressure walk and the Lagrangian agree, term by term. The page uses ptop=p2 as a live self-test; the error stays below 10−10 Pa.

In our press
With a 5 mm pipe, A1/Ap=64 and the oil in the pipe reaches 23.5 m/s just before the stop. The Bernoulli drop 12ρvp2≈240 kPa is larger than the 150 kPa in the oil, and the gauge pressure at the pipe outlet falls to −96 kPa: the oil there is under suction, which is why the page warns about cavitation.

6 What “ideal” leaves out

The stops in the first animation are perfectly rigid and perfectly inelastic: the speed drops to zero in zero time and the kinetic energy 12μv2 disappears. That needs an infinite force. With real oil of bulk modulus β the stop is a stiff spring; Part II builds exactly that. A rough estimate of the pressure spike: the moving mass μ hitting an oil spring of stiffness ko=βA12/V carries 12μv2=12koδ2 into it, so the peak force is vkoμ and

Δppeak≈vkoμA1.(6.1)

Take all 5.1 L of oil as the spring volume V, β=1.5 GPa and the impact speed v=0.67 m/s: this is about 1.5 MPa, ten times the working pressure, ringing at ko/μ/2π≈25 Hz. Two more losses are missing. Where the jet leaves the pipe into the wide column it does not slow down gently: it mixes, and the Borda–Carnot loss 12ρ(vp−rv)2 is burnt as heat. And viscosity resists every flow; chapter 11 derives how much.

Part IIThe servo press: building the model from the parts

7 Oil is a stiff spring

7.1 The bulk modulus

Seal a syringe full of oil and lean on the plunger with all your weight: it moves by a hair. The oil is not incompressible, only very stiff.

The bulk modulus measures how much pressure it takes to squeeze a volume V by a fraction dV/V:

β=−VdpdV=ρdpdρ.(7.1)

The second form follows because the mass ρV of a sealed sample is fixed, so dρ/ρ=−dV/V. Mineral hydraulic oil has β≈1.5 GPa: squeezing it by 1 % takes 15 MPa.

7.2 The pressure build-up equation

This is the most important equation in hydraulics. Take a chamber whose volume V(t) can change because a piston of area A moves out at speed y˙, so V˙=Ay˙. Oil flows in at Qin and out at Qout (Figure 2). The mass inside is m=ρV. Mass is conserved, so it changes only through the flows:

ddt(ρV)=ρQin−ρQout.

Expand the left side with the product rule, and use (7.1) to replace ρ˙ by (ρ/β)p˙:

ρV˙+Vρ˙=ρV˙+ρVβp˙=ρ(Qin−Qout).

Divide by ρ:

Vβp˙=Qin−Qout−V˙(7.2)

Read it as a budget: the net flow into the chamber either moves the boundary (V˙) or compresses the oil already there (Vp˙/β). The ratio C=V/β is the hydraulic capacitance; it plays the role of a capacitor, with pressure as voltage and flow as current. Every chamber of the servo press obeys (7.2).

Check
Units: m3/Pa·Pa/s=m3/s. Limit β→∞: the left side vanishes and Qin−Qout=V˙, the incompressible continuity of chapter 2.

7.3 Air in the oil

A few bubbles turn the stiff spring into a soft one. Bubbles are springs that stiffen as you squeeze them: crush them to a fortieth of their size and they barely matter.

Take a mixture of a liquid volume Vℓ and a gas volume Vg. When the pressure rises by dp both shrink. The liquid follows (7.1): dVℓ=−Vℓdp/βoil. The gas follows a polytropic law pabsVgn=const, whose differential is npabsVgn−1dVg+Vgndp=0, so dVg=−Vgdp/(npabs). The compliances add:

dVdp=−Vℓβoil−Vgnpabs.

Apply the definition (7.1) to the mixture, βeff=−(Vℓ+Vg)/(dV/dp), and divide top and bottom by Vℓ, writing α=Vg/Vℓ:

βeff=1+α1βoil+αnpabs=βoil1+α1+αβoilnpabs.(7.3)

The gas volume itself shrinks with pressure. If the air makes up a fraction α0 of the total volume at atmospheric pressure p0, then Vg/Vℓ=α0/(1−α0) there, and the polytropic law gives

α(p)=α01−α0(p0pabs)1/n.(7.4)

βeff, 0.5 % air28 MPa87 MPa428 MPa795 MPa1.16 GPa1.39 GPa1.46 GPa1.48 GPa

At zero gauge pressure half a percent of air makes the oil fifty times softer. Near vacuum (pabs→0) the effective modulus goes to zero, which in the model also stops the pressure from falling below absolute zero.

8 Flow through a sharp-edged orifice

Squeeze a garden hose with your thumb and the water shoots out fast: pressure turned into speed. In the valve, the speed is then lost in a turbulent swirl, and the pressure difference becomes heat.

Upstream of the opening the oil is almost still at pressure pu. It accelerates through the gap and the jet keeps contracting a little beyond the edge, to its narrowest section, the vena contracta, where the streamlines are parallel and the pressure equals the downstream pressure pd. Apply steady Bernoulli, (5.2) without the time term, between a point upstream and the vena contracta:

pu=pd+12ρvj2⇒vj=2(pu−pd)ρ.

The jet area is a fraction Cc of the opening Ao (about 0.61 for a sharp edge), and friction reduces the speed slightly. Both factors are lumped into a discharge coefficient Cd≈0.6 to 0.7:

Q=CdAo2Δpρ,Δp=pu−pd.(8.1)

In a spool valve the opening is a slot: Ao=wx, with w the circumferential width of the port (the area gradient) and x the spool travel. Normalize the travel to x^=x/xmax∈[−1,1] and collect the constants into one valve coefficient:

Q=Kvx^Δp,Kv=Cdwxmax2/ρ.(8.2)

Manufacturers quote a rated flow QN at a rated drop ΔpN across the whole valve, which is two metering edges in series, each taking ΔpN/2. Hence Kv=QN/ΔpN/2.

In our press
QN=100 L/min at 7 MPa gives Kv=8.91×10−7 m3/(sPa). With Cd=0.62 that is a full opening wxmax=30 mm2: a 10 mm diameter port ring opened by about 1 mm.

8.1 Taming the square root

The square root has an infinite slope at Δp=0, which a linear analysis and a numerical integrator both dislike. Real orifices also become laminar at tiny flows, where Q∝Δp. The model uses

g(Δp)=Δp(Δp2+ptr2)1/4≈{Δp/ptr,|Δp|≪ptr[4pt]sign(Δp)|Δp|,|Δp|≫ptr(8.3)

with ptr=0.1 MPa. It is linear near zero, square-root far away, and smooth everywhere. It also handles flow in the reverse direction through the sign of Δp.

8.2 Leakage

Between the lands and the bore there is a radial clearance of a few micrometres. Flow through such a gap is laminar, so it is proportional to the pressure difference (chapter 11 derives this for a pipe): QL=KLΔp, with KL=2×10−13 m3/(sPa).

9 The four-way valve

A four-way valve has four metering edges. For xv>0 the edges P→A and B→T open; for xv<0 the edges P→B and A→T open. The valve is critically lapped: at xv=0 all four edges are exactly closed and only leakage flows. Writing x+=max(x^,0) and x−=min(x^,0), the flows out of the ports are

QA=Kv[x+g(ps−pa)+x−g(pa−pT)]+KL(ps−2pa+pT)QB=−Kv[x+g(pB−pT)+x−g(ps−pB)]+KL(ps−2pB+pT)QS=Kv[x+g(ps−pa)−x−g(ps−pB)]+KL(2ps−pa−pB).(9.1)

Each line follows from (8.2) and (8.3) applied to the open edges, plus leakage from the pump into each port and from each port to the tank.

9.1 Where the power goes

The pump delivers hydraulic power psQS. The ports deliver paQA+pBQB to the actuator, and the tank receives pT(QS−QA−QB). The difference is lost in the jets as heat:

Pvalve=psQS−paQA−pBQB−pT(QS−QA−QB).(9.2)
In our press
Over one cycle the pump does 26.3 kJ of work and the valve burns 24.4 kJ of it: 93 %. A valve throttling a constant-pressure supply is a brake. Industrial presses use variable pumps or accumulators to waste less.

9.2 The two valve coefficients

For small changes around a steady operating point, expand QA(xv,pa) to first order:

δQA=Kqδxv−Kcδpa,Kq=∂QA∂xv,Kc=−∂QA∂pa.(9.3)

Kq is the flow gain and Kc the flow-pressure coefficient. For xv>0 and |Δp|≫ptr, (9.1) gives Kq=Kvps−pa and Kc=Kvxv/(2ps−pa)+2KL. The term Kc acts like a resistor that bleeds flow when the pressure rises; it supplies most of the damping of an open valve.

In our press
Cruising up at 0.1 m/s, the spool is 40.6 % open with ps−pa=9.76 MPa: Kq=2.8×10−3 m3/s per unit spool and Kc=5.8×10−11 m3/(sPa), about 145 times the leakage.

10 The spool and its torque motor

A current in the torque motor tilts an armature; in a two-stage valve the armature steers a small pilot flow that pushes the main spool. The details are a chapter of their own, but measured responses of good servo valves are described well by a second-order system from the command u to the spool position:

x¨v+2ζvωvx˙v+ωv2xv=ωv2u,ωv=2πfv.(10.1)

Its frequency response, from u=u^ejωt, is

xvu(jω)=11−(ω/ωv)2+2jζvω/ωv,φ(ω)=−arctan2ζvω/ωv1−(ω/ωv)2.(10.2)
In our press
fv=60 Hz, ζv=0.7. At 10 Hz the spool lags by 13°, at 38 Hz already by 56°: the valve alone eats a good part of the phase a feedback loop can afford.

11 The hose: a slug of oil

11.1 Inertance

Swing the end of a garden hose full of water: the water lags behind, then pushes back. The column has mass, and a narrow bore makes it hard to get moving, because every litre per second must run fast.

Treat the oil in a hose of length Lh and cross-section Ah as one rigid slug of mass ρAhLh, moving at speed Qh/Ah. The pressure at the inlet pushes it forward with paAh, the pressure at the outlet pushes it back with pAAh, and friction costs a pressure drop Δpf. Newton's second law:

ρAhLhQ˙hAh=(pa−pA−Δpf)Ah⇒IQ˙h=pa−pA−Δpf,I=ρLhAh.(11.1)

I is the inertance, the hydraulic analogue of an inductor. It grows as the bore shrinks, I∝1/dh2: a narrow hose is a heavy hose.

In our press
2 m of 12 mm hose: I=1.54×107 kg/m4. To accelerate the flow by 10 L/min in 10 ms takes
IQ˙=1.54×107×1.67×10−40.01=0.26 MPa.

11.2 Laminar friction: Hagen–Poiseuille

In slow, steady flow the layers of oil slide over each other. Take a cylinder of oil of radius r and length L, coaxial with the pipe. The pressure difference pushes its ends with Δpπr2. On its curved surface the shear stress τ=−μdu/dr of the slower outer oil holds it back with τ2πrL. In steady flow these balance:

Δpπr2=−μdudr2πrL⇒dudr=−Δp2μLr.

Integrate with the no-slip condition u(R)=0 at the wall:

u(r)=Δp4μL(R2−r2).

Add up the flow through thin rings of area 2πrdr:

Q=∫0Ru(r)2πrdr=πΔp2μL[R2r22−r44]0R=πR48μLΔp.

With R=d/2:

Δp=RlamQ,Rlam=128μLπd4.(11.2)
Check
The fourth power is brutal: halve the bore and the resistance rises sixteen times. Units: Pas·m/m4=Pas/m3, a pressure per flow.

11.3 Turbulent friction

Whether the flow stays laminar depends on the Reynolds number Re=u¯d/ν, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. Above about 2300 the flow becomes turbulent, and the pressure drop grows with the square of the speed, as in the Darcy–Weisbach law plus the losses in fittings and bends:

Δp=(fLd+Km)12ρu¯2=KtQ|Q|,Kt=ρ(fL/d+Km)2A2.(11.3)

The model adds the two, Δpf=RlamQ+KtQ|Q|: the laminar term dominates at small flow, the turbulent term at large flow. The form Q|Q| keeps the sign of the loss opposite to the flow.

In our press
At 60 L/min in the 12 mm hose the mean speed is 8.8 m/s and Re≈3300: turbulent. Rlam=1.09×108 Pas/m3, Kt=1.64×1011 kg/m7 with f=0.02 and Km=1.5.

11.4 When is one slug enough?

Pressure waves run along the hose at c=β/ρ≈1300 m/s. A 2 m hose rings like an organ pipe at about c/2L≈330 Hz and above. One rigid slug is a good model only well below that. The servo loop crosses over near 10 Hz, so the lumped model is good where the controller works. The 174 Hz mode it predicts, however, is already halfway to the first wave mode, so treat its exact frequency with care.

12 Chambers, ram, friction, contact

12.1 The three pressure equations

Apply the build-up equation (7.2) three times. The small volume at the valve port, Va, which includes half the hose, receives QA and sends Qh down the hose. The cap chamber receives Qh and grows as the ram rises. The rod chamber receives QB and shrinks as the ram rises:

Vaβ(pa)p˙a=QA−QhVA(y)β(pA)p˙A=Qh−AAy˙,VA=VA0+12Vh+AAyVB(y)β(pB)p˙B=QB+ABy˙,VB=VB0+AB(S−y)(12.1)

The cap area is the full bore, AA=πD2/4; the rod side has the annulus AB=π(D2−dr2)/4. Each chamber carries its own β(p) from (7.3), because each sits at its own pressure.

12.2 Newton for the ram

my¨=pAAA−pBAB−mg−Ff(y˙)−Fw(y,y˙)+Fstop(y,y˙).(12.2)

The cap pressure pushes up on the full bore, the rod pressure pushes down on the annulus, gravity pulls down, and three contact forces remain.

12.3 Seal friction

Push a heavy box across a floor: it refuses to move until you push hard enough, jerks loose, then slides with less effort. Piston seals do the same.

The model combines three effects. A Coulomb force Fc opposes sliding; at low speeds a higher break-away force kstFc (the Stribeck effect) has to be overcome; and a viscous term bvy˙ grows with speed. The jump of the Coulomb force at y˙=0 is smoothed with a hyperbolic tangent over a speed vs=1 mm/s:

Ff(y˙)=Fctanh(y˙vs)[1+(kst−1)e−(y˙/vstr)2]+bvy˙.(12.3)

The smoothing makes the equations integrable with a fixed step. Its price is that at rest the friction behaves like an enormous damper of slope Fckst/vs≈106 Ns/m; problem 4 on the press page shows how that misleads a linear analysis.

12.4 The specimen and the stop

The specimen is a spring kw with a damper cw in parallel (a Kelvin–Voigt solid). It is only touched when the platen has climbed past yc, and it can push but not pull:

Fw={max(0, kwδ+cwδ˙),δ=y−yc>00,otherwise(12.4)

The bottom stop is the same law with a very stiff spring, ks=2×108 N/m, acting when y<0.

13 The complete model

Collect the state: spool position and speed, the three pressures, the hose flow, ram position and speed,

𝐱=(xv, x˙v, pa, Qh, pA, pB, y, y˙).(13.1)

Equations (10.1), (12.1), (11.1) and (12.2), with the valve flows (9.1), friction (12.3) and contact (12.4), form a system 𝐱˙=𝐟(𝐱,u) of eight first-order nonlinear differential equations driven by one input, the command u. That is all the servo press is. The page integrates it with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 100 steps per controller sample, and integrates seven more quantities alongside to keep the energy books (chapter 19).

14 Operating points and linearization

14.1 Steady operation

An operating point is a state 𝐱0 and input u0 at which nothing changes except, possibly, the ram position, which may drift at a constant speed v0. Setting the right-hand sides of section 13 to zero gives three conditions: the valve delivers exactly the flow the moving ram needs on each side, QA=AAv0 and QB=−ABv0; and the forces on the ram balance,

pAAA−pBAB=mg+Ff(v0)+Fw.

Three equations, three unknowns (xv,pA,pB): the page solves them with Newton's method.

In our press
Cruising up at 0.1 m/s at y=60 mm: xv=40.6%, pA=1.91 MPa, pB=3.03 MPa. Holding 60 kN: xv=0.039%, pA=7.79 MPa, pB=4.21 MPa. At rest the critically lapped valve balances the leakages with the spool just off centre.

14.2 Small deviations

Write 𝐱=𝐱0+δ𝐱 and u=u0+δu, and expand 𝐟 to first order in a Taylor series:

δ𝐱˙=𝐟(𝐱0,u0)+∂𝐟∂𝐱⏟Aδ𝐱+∂𝐟∂u⏟Bδu+O(‖δ𝐱‖2).(14.1)

At an operating point 𝐟(𝐱0,u0) is zero, apart from the constant drift v0 in the position row, which does not affect the deviations. What remains is a linear system δ𝐱˙=Aδ𝐱+Bδu with an output δyout=Cδ𝐱: C picks the ram position for the position loop, and kwδy+cwδy˙ for the force loop. The page computes A and B by central differences,

Aij≈fi(𝐱0+h𝐞j)−fi(𝐱0−h𝐞j)2h,(14.2)

whose error is of order h2; the check script compares them with exact symbolic derivatives and finds agreement to 10−9 or better.

Where linearization fails
The derivative must exist. The critically lapped valve has a kink at xv=0, where the flow gain jumps from Kvpa to Kvps−pa, and the friction law has a near-jump at y˙=0. Around such points a linear model describes only one side; problems 3 and 4 explore what follows.

15 The classical valve-controlled cylinder

Before trusting a computer with an eight-state model, it pays to derive the textbook three-state version by hand. Take a symmetric cylinder of area A with equal chamber volumes Vt/2, no hose, a symmetric valve, and define the load pressure pL=p1−p2 and the load flow QL=(Q1+Q2)/2. Linearized, the valve gives

QL=Kqxv−KcpL.(15.1)

Write the build-up equation (7.2) for each chamber, (Vt/2β)p˙1=Q1−Ay˙−CtppL and (Vt/2β)p˙2=−Q2+Ay˙+CtppL, where Ctp is the leakage across the piston. Subtract the second from the first and divide by 2:

QL=Ay˙+CtppL+Vt4βp˙L.(15.2)

Newton for the ram of mass m with viscous friction b and no external load: my¨=ApL−by˙. Take Laplace transforms with zero initial conditions and eliminate QL and pL. From (15.1) and (15.2), with Kce=Kc+Ctp:

PL(s)=KqXv(s)−AsY(s)Kce+Vt4βs.

Insert into (ms2+bs)Y=APL, multiply out, and collect:

Y(s)Xv(s)=Kq/As[Vtm4βA2s2+(KcemA2+bVt4βA2)s+1+bKceA2].(15.3)

The last term bKce/A2 is tiny in practice. Dropping it leaves the classic form

Y(s)Xv(s)=Kq/As(s2ωh2+2ζhωhs+1),ωh2=4βA2Vtm,ζh=KceAβmVt+b4AVtβm(15.4)

Read it as two pieces. The integrator 1/s: a valve opening produces a flow, and a flow produces a speed, not a position. And a resonance at ωh: the ram bouncing on the oil springs of its two chambers. For the differential cylinder of the press, the two springs differ, and the same reasoning gives

ωh2=1m(βAAA2VA+βBAB2VB).(15.5)
In our press
With both ports closed, (15.5) gives 245 Hz near the bottom of the stroke, 213 Hz near the top and a minimum of 170 Hz at 115 mm. With the hose attached, the full model's pole table shows the ram at 88 Hz with damping 0.26 at the cruise point: the hose slug and the extra volume soften and couple the resonance.

The shape of (15.4) explains the most famous rule of thumb in hydraulic servo design. A proportional position loop around an integrator has loop gain Kv/s, where Kv is the velocity gain. It stays stable only if the loop gain has fallen well below 1 by the time the resonance lifts it again, which gives approximately Kv<2ζhωh. Problem 9 shows why the real limit is lower.

16 From continuous to discrete: sample and hold

16.1 Solving the linear system exactly

A digital controller sees the plant only at the sampling instants tk=kTs and holds its command constant in between (a zero-order hold). For a linear system we can then step from one sample to the next exactly. Multiply 𝐱˙=A𝐱+Bu by the integrating factor e−At:

ddt(e−At𝐱)=e−At(𝐱˙−A𝐱)=e−AtBu.

Integrate from tk to tk+1=tk+Ts, with u=uk constant, and substitute σ=tk+1−τ:

𝐱k+1=eATs𝐱k+∫tktk+1eA(tk+1−τ)dτBuk=Φ𝐱k+Γuk,Φ=eATs,Γ=∫0TseAσdσB.(16.1)

16.2 One exponential gives both

Treat the held input as an extra state that does not change: u˙=0. The augmented system is

ddt[𝐱u]=[AB00]⏟M[𝐱u]⇒eMTs=[ΦΓ01].(16.2)

Comparing with (16.1) shows the top-right block is exactly Γ. The page computes eMTs by scaling and squaring: it divides MTs by 2s until its norm is below one half, sums the Taylor series, and squares the result s times. SciPy's expm agrees to 10−14.

16.3 The z-transform

Let z stand for “one sample later”, so that 𝐱k+1 becomes z𝐱. Then (16.1) gives 𝐱=(zI−Φ)−1Γu and the discrete transfer function from command to output is

G(z)=C(zI−Φ)−1Γ.(16.3)

A continuous pole s becomes a discrete pole z=esTs. The left half-plane, where continuous systems are stable, maps into the unit disc: a sampled system is stable when every pole satisfies |z|<1. A sinusoid of angular frequency ω corresponds to z=ejωTs, a point on the unit circle; frequencies run up to the Nyquist frequency π/Ts, which is 500 Hz at Ts=1 ms.

16.4 What the hold costs in phase

A hold that keeps each value for one period has the impulse response of a rectangle of width Ts. Its transfer function, normalized to unit gain at zero frequency, is (1−e−sTs)/(sTs). On the imaginary axis, factor out half a period:

1−e−jωTsjωTs=e−jωTs/2ejωTs/2−e−jωTs/2jωTs=e−jωTs/2sin(ωTs/2)ωTs/2.(16.4)

The hold delays the signal by half a sample: a phase lag of ωTs/2. The press's controller also needs one sample to compute, a further lag of ωTs. Together:

φsampling=1.5ωTs.(16.5)

At the 9.7 Hz crossover this is 5.2° with Ts=1 ms and 21.0° with Ts=4 ms. The measured phase margin falls by 15.8°, almost exactly the difference.

17 The digital controller

17.1 PID in discrete time

A continuous PID controller acts on the error e=yref−y as u=Kpe+Ki∫edt+Kde˙. In a computer the integral becomes a running sum. The backward Euler rule approximates ∫0tkedt by adding Tsek each sample:

Ik=Ik−1+KiTsek⇒I(z)E(z)=KiTszz−1.(17.1)

A pure derivative amplifies noise without limit, so it is filtered: D(s)=Kds/(Tfs+1) with Tf=2 ms. Backward Euler replaces s by (1−z−1)/Ts:

D(z)=Kd1−z−1TsTf1−z−1Ts+1=Kd(z−1)(Tf+Ts)z−Tf.(17.2)

In the time domain this is the line the page runs:

Dk=TfTf+TsDk−1+KdTf+Ts(ek−ek−1).

The position controller is the sum of the three parts, and the force controller is a PI:

K(z)=Kp+KiTszz−1+D(z),KF(z)=KpF+KiFTszz−1.

17.2 Feed-forward

Feedback corrects errors after they appear. If the controller knows the speed it is asking for, it can open the valve by the right amount in advance. From (8.2), a spool opening x^ against roughly half the supply pressure produces a flow Kvx^ps/2 and a speed Kvx^ps/2/AA. Inverting:

uff=Kffvref,Kff=AAKvps/2.(17.3)

The feedback then only has to correct what the feed-forward gets wrong.

17.3 Saturation and windup

The spool cannot open more than fully: u is clipped to [−1,1]. While it is clipped, the error persists and a naive integrator keeps growing; when the error finally reverses, the oversized integrator holds the valve open far too long. This is windup. The page uses conditional integration: it accepts the new integral only if the output is unsaturated, or if the error would drive it back out of saturation.

17.4 Switching laws without a bump

When the supervisor switches from position to force control, the command must not jump. At the switch the new error is zero (the force reference starts at the measured force), so u=KpF·0+IF. Setting IF=uprevious makes the command continuous. Problem 8 removes this and measures a 2.7 kN stumble.

17.5 The supervisor

A small state machine decides which law runs: settle holds position for 0.1 s; approach follows a quintic profile, s(τ)=10τ3−15τ4+6τ5, whose speed and acceleration are zero at both ends; creep rises at 10 mm/s until the load cell reads more than 1 kN; press ramps the force to its target and holds it; release ramps it down; retract returns on another quintic; done holds.

18 Is it stable? Closed loop, Nyquist, margins

18.1 The closed-loop matrix

Write the controller as a discrete state-space system from its input (the error) to its output (the command): 𝐬k+1=Ac𝐬k+Bcek and uk=Cc𝐬k+Dcek. Add one state wk for the command that is still waiting one sample to reach the valve. With ek=−C𝐱k for deviations from the reference:

[𝐱𝐬w]k+1=[Φ0Γ−BcCAc0−DcCCc0][𝐱𝐬w]k.(18.1)

The loop is stable if and only if every eigenvalue of this matrix lies inside the unit circle. The page computes them with a balanced Hessenberg QR algorithm; NumPy agrees to 10−15.

18.2 The loop gain and the Nyquist plot

Cut the loop at the controller output and follow a sinusoid around it: through the delay z−1, the plant G(z) and the controller K(z). The ratio of what comes back to what went in is the loop gain

L(z)=K(z)z−1G(z),z=ejωTs.(18.2)

The closed loop amplifies by 1/(1+L), so trouble comes when L approaches −1: a signal returns inverted with the same size and reinforces itself. Nyquist's criterion makes this exact. Plot L(ejωTs) as ω runs once around; the number of clockwise encirclements of −1 equals the number of unstable closed-loop poles minus the number of unstable open-loop poles. The integrators of our loops sit exactly on the unit circle, at z=1; the contour goes around them on a small half-circle, which is why the curve comes in from infinity.

18.3 Gain and phase margins

Two numbers summarize how far the curve stays from −1. The phase margin is how much extra lag the loop tolerates at the crossover frequency, where |L|=1: PM=180∘+∠L. The gain margin is by how much the gain may grow at the frequency where the phase reaches −180∘: GM=−20log10|L| in decibels. Good designs keep PM≳45∘ and GM≳6 dB.

In our press
Position loop: 64.0° and 11.0 dB, crossover 9.7 Hz. Force loop: 49.5° and 10.6 dB, crossover 9.2 Hz. Raise the position gain to 90 %/mm and the closed-loop poles leave the unit circle.

19 Where the energy goes

Multiply each equation of the model by its “flow” partner and add them. The pump power psQS splits as follows. The valve keeps (9.2). Each chamber turns p(Qin−Qout−V˙) into compression energy; by (7.2) that is p(V/β)p˙. The hose slug gains ddt(12IQh2) and loses ΔpfQh to friction. The ram gains kinetic energy ddt(12my˙2) and height mgy˙, and loses Ffy˙ to the seals and Fwy˙ to the specimen. Summing every term, all the internal pressures cancel in pairs:

psQS=Pvalve+∑chamberspVβp˙+ddt(12IQh2+12my˙2+mgy)+ΔpfQh+Ffy˙+Fwy˙−Fstopy˙.(19.1)

The page integrates every term separately and checks the sum against the pump work at each sample. The residual stays at 10−14 of the pump work: a test that every force in the mechanics matches its flow partner in the hydraulics.

20 How the computer solves it

Both pages use the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method. From 𝐱n it samples the slope four times across a step h and averages them with weights 1, 2, 2, 1:

𝐤1=𝐟(𝐱n),𝐤2=𝐟(𝐱n+h2𝐤1),𝐤3=𝐟(𝐱n+h2𝐤2),𝐤4=𝐟(𝐱n+h𝐤3),𝐱n+1=𝐱n+h6(𝐤1+2𝐤2+2𝐤3+𝐤4).(20.1)

The local error is of order h5 and the global error of order h4: halving the step cuts the error sixteen-fold. Applied to the test equation x˙=λx, one step multiplies x by

R(λh)=1+λh+(λh)22+(λh)36+(λh)424.

The method is stable while |R|≤1, which on the negative real axis means λh≥−2.79.

In our press
The fastest mode of the servo model is the small valve-port volume charged through a wide-open valve while the pressure drop across it is small, where the rounded orifice law (8.3) is steepest: λ≈βKv/(Vaptr)≈1.6×104 s−1. With h=10−5 s, λh≈0.16, far inside the stable region. Stops are located inside a step by bisection on the step length, to 10−12 s.
