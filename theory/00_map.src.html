0 The machine, before any equation

0.1 A machine for multiplying forces

In a treatise written in 1653 and printed in 1663, a year after his death, Blaise Pascal drew a sealed vessel full of water with two openings, each closed by a piston, and gave the figure a title: Nouvelle sorte de Machine pour multiplier les forces, a new kind of machine for multiplying forces. His text explains it in one sentence:

« Si un Vaisseau plein d’eau, clos de toutes parts, a deux ouvertures, l’une centuple de l’autre : en mettant à chacune un Piston qui luy soit juste, un homme poussant le petit Piston égalera la force de cent hommes, qui pousseront celuy qui est cent fois plus large, et en surmontera quatre vingt dix neuf. »

If a vessel full of water, closed on all sides, has two openings, one a hundred times the other, and each is fitted with a piston that fits it exactly, one man pushing the small piston will equal the force of a hundred men pushing the piston a hundred times larger, and will overcome ninety-nine of them.

Blaise Pascal, Traité de l’équilibre des liqueurs (1663), chapter II, figure VII. Text from Œuvres de Blaise Pascal, vol. III, p. 162, Wikisource. The editors note, after Duhem, that Benedetti had come close to the same device in 1585.

Pascal did not build it. Joseph Bramah did: he was granted a patent for his hydraulic press in 1795, after studying the existing literature on the motion of fluids while he improved the flush toilet. He and William Armstrong are remembered as the two pioneers of industrial hydraulics, and the machine is still called the Bramah press. Part I of this text is Pascal’s and Bramah’s machine, made to move.

Why oil, and why not a lever? Because a fluid carries force around corners, through a pipe of any length and shape, and because it carries a great deal of it in a small space. At the 12 MPa of the servo press in Parts II and III, every square centimetre of piston feels 1.2 kN, the weight of a small car. The 12 cm piston of that press pushes with 136 kN, while the pipe that feeds it is 12 mm across. No gear train or cable of that size comes close.

The price is control. A fluid under pressure is also a spring, and a column of fluid in a hose is also a mass, so a press driven by oil can ring, overshoot and shake itself apart if it is commanded carelessly. The servo press of Parts II and III adds the valve and the computer that keep it in check. In 1950 W. C. Moog Jr. filed the patent for the first two-stage electrohydraulic servovalve, in which a small torque motor moves a flapper that steers a larger spool. Valves of that family still fly aircraft, steer ships and drive the materials-testing presses that this one is modelled on.

0.2 Blocks, inputs and outputs

Squeeze a sealed plastic bottle full of water: you push, and the pressure inside answers. You cannot choose both how hard you push and how high the pressure goes; you choose one, and the bottle decides the other.

Every part of a hydraulic machine works like that bottle. It is a block: a piece of hardware with a law inside it. Its inputs are what the rest of the machine imposes on it. Its outputs are what it answers with, computed from its inputs and from its own state, such as how much oil it holds or how fast it moves. A block is never a black box here. Each one is opened in its own chapter, and its law is derived there from Newton’s laws and the conservation of mass and energy.

The connections between blocks come in pairs. Wherever oil passes from one part to another it carries a pressure $p$ and a flow $Q$, and their product is a power:

$$P = p\,Q, \qquad \mathrm{Pa}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{m^3}}{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{m^2}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{m^3}}{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\mathrm{N\,m}}{\mathrm{s}} = \mathrm{W}.$$

Wherever a part pushes on a moving part, the pair is a force $F$ and a speed $v$, with $P = Fv$. In each pair one quantity flows one way through the diagram and the other comes back. Which way each goes is decided by the physics of the block, and there are only three kinds of hydraulic block:

  • A volume of oil, such as a cylinder chamber, takes in flows and answers with a pressure. Oil is slightly compressible, so the pressure rises as the net inflow packs oil in. It behaves like an electrical capacitor, with pressure as the voltage and flow as the current (chapter 7).
  • A column of moving oil, such as the oil inside a long hose, takes in a pressure difference and answers with a flow. The pressure difference accelerates the column like any mass. It behaves like an inductor (chapter 11).
  • A restriction, such as the metering edge of a valve or a narrow pipe, also takes a pressure difference and answers with a flow, but at once, by friction or by turning pressure into the speed of a jet. It behaves like a resistor, a nonlinear one for a sharp edge (chapters 8 and 11).

Mechanical parts follow the same pattern: a mass takes in forces and answers with a speed (Newton), a spring takes in a displacement and answers with a force (Hooke). Once you know which of these each block is, you know which way its arrows point, and the whole machine becomes a chain of answers.

Two things in this table are worth a second look. First, no block is given what it answers with: the chambers never decide a flow, the hose never decides a pressure. The ram’s position goes through the sensors to the controller, which moves the spool, which changes the flows, which change the pressures, which push the ram. Part III is about what that loop does to itself: whether a disturbance dies away or grows.

0.4 One press cycle, told in words

The animation of the servo press runs one cycle of about 4.4 s. Here is what each block does in it.

  1. Settle, 0.1 s. The controller holds the ram at $y = 0$. The spool sits almost exactly in the middle: just enough off centre that the small leakage flows into and out of each chamber balance.
  2. Approach, 1 s. The controller asks for a smooth climb to 2 mm short of the specimen, 118 mm up. To lift the ram it opens the spool positively: P→A and B→T open. Oil runs from the pump down the hose into the cap chamber, the cap pressure rises just enough to carry the ram’s weight and the seal friction, and the oil above the piston is pushed out to the tank.
  3. Creep, about 0.2 s. The ram rises slowly, at 10 mm/s, until the load cell reads 1 kN: it has touched the specimen.
  4. Press, 0.75 s. The controller switches from position to force. It ramps the force to 60 kN in 0.25 s and holds it for 0.5 s. The ram hardly moves now, about 3 mm into a specimen that is a stiff spring. The ram pushes up into the specimen, so the specimen pushes down on the ram, and so does the ram’s weight. Holding 60 kN needs $p_A = 7.8\ \mathrm{MPa}$ under the piston against $p_B = 4.2\ \mathrm{MPa}$ above it: $$\begin{aligned} &7.8\ \mathrm{MPa} \times 113\ \mathrm{cm^2} - 4.2\ \mathrm{MPa} \times 63\ \mathrm{cm^2}\\ &\qquad \approx 88.1\ \mathrm{kN} - 26.5\ \mathrm{kN} = 61.6\ \mathrm{kN}, \end{aligned}$$ which is the 60 kN on the specimen plus the 1.6 kN weight of the ram.
  5. Release, 0.25 s. The force ramps back down to 1 kN.
  6. Retract, 1.8 s. Back to position control, and a smooth descent to $y = 0$: the spool opens negatively, P→B and A→T, and oil drains from the cap chamber back up the hose.
  7. Done, 0.3 s. The ram holds at the bottom.

Every number in that story comes out of the equations of chapters 7 to 17, integrated by the page. The chapters derive those equations in the order in which oil meets the parts: first the ideal press, where nothing is compressible and nothing leaks, then the real one.

0.5 How to read the rest

Every equation the two animations integrate is derived here, line by line, from Newton’s laws, conservation of mass and conservation of energy. You need single-variable calculus, a little vector calculus and, for Part III, the Laplace transform and some linear algebra.

Each derivation is built the same way. A picture tells you what to imagine with your hands and eyes. A block card says what the part is given and what it answers with. The working is the mathematics, one step per line. A check tests the result against units and limiting cases. In our press puts in the numbers the animation uses, so you can compare with what you see on screen.

Conventions
  • SI units throughout. A pressure written $p$ is a gauge pressure, measured above the atmosphere; the absolute pressure is $p_{\mathrm{abs}} = p + p_0$ with $p_0 = 101.325\ \mathrm{kPa}$.
  • A dot is a time derivative, $\dot{x} = dx/dt$. A prime is a derivative with respect to a position coordinate, $\mu' = d\mu/dx$.
  • Flows $Q$ are volume flows in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$; one litre per minute is $1.667\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$.
  • Numbers quoted “in our press” are for the default settings of the pages.
