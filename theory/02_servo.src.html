Part IIThe servo press: building the model from the parts

7 Oil is a stiff spring

7.1 The bulk modulus

Seal a syringe full of oil and lean on the plunger with all your weight: it moves by a hair. The oil is not incompressible, only very stiff.

The bulk modulus measures how much pressure it takes to squeeze a volume $V$ by a fraction $dV/V$:

$$\beta = -V\,\frac{dp}{dV} = \rho\,\frac{dp}{d\rho}. \tag{7.1}$$

The second form follows because the mass $\rho V$ of a sealed sample is fixed, so $d\rho/\rho = -dV/V$. Mineral hydraulic oil has $\beta \approx 1.5\ \mathrm{GPa}$: squeezing it by 1 % takes 15 MPa.

7.2 The pressure build-up equation

This is the most important equation in hydraulics. Take a chamber whose volume $V(t)$ can change because a piston of area $A$ moves out at speed $\dot{y}$, so $\dot{V} = A\dot{y}$. Oil flows in at $Q_{\mathrm{in}}$ and out at $Q_{\mathrm{out}}$ (Figure 2). The mass inside is $m = \rho V$. Mass is conserved, so it changes only through the flows:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho V\right) = \rho\,Q_{\mathrm{in}} - \rho\,Q_{\mathrm{out}}.$$

Expand the left side with the product rule, and use (7.1) to replace $\dot{\rho}$ by $(\rho/\beta)\dot{p}$:

$$\rho\dot{V} + V\dot{\rho} = \rho\dot{V} + \frac{\rho V}{\beta}\dot{p} = \rho\left(Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}}\right).$$

Divide by $\rho$:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{V}{\beta}\,\dot{p} = Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}} - \dot{V}\;} \tag{7.2}$$

Read it as a budget: the net flow into the chamber either moves the boundary ($\dot V$) or compresses the oil already there ($V\dot p/\beta$). The ratio $C = V/\beta$ is the hydraulic capacitance; it plays the role of a capacitor, with pressure as voltage and flow as current. Every chamber of the servo press obeys (7.2).

Check
Units: $\mathrm{m^3/Pa}\cdot\mathrm{Pa/s} = \mathrm{m^3/s}$. Limit $\beta\to\infty$: the left side vanishes and $Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}} = \dot{V}$, the incompressible continuity of chapter 2.

7.3 Air in the oil

A few bubbles turn the stiff spring into a soft one. Bubbles are springs that stiffen as you squeeze them: crush them to a fortieth of their size and they barely matter.

Take a mixture of a liquid volume $V_\ell$ and a gas volume $V_g$. When the pressure rises by $dp$ both shrink. The liquid follows (7.1): $dV_\ell = -V_\ell\,dp/\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}$. The gas follows a polytropic law $p_{\mathrm{abs}}V_g^n = \text{const}$, whose differential is $n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}V_g^{n-1}dV_g + V_g^n\,dp = 0$, so $dV_g = -V_g\,dp/(n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}})$. The compliances add:

$$\frac{dV}{dp} = -\frac{V_\ell}{\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}} - \frac{V_g}{n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}}.$$

Apply the definition (7.1) to the mixture, $\beta_{\mathrm{eff}} = -(V_\ell + V_g)/(dV/dp)$, and divide top and bottom by $V_\ell$, writing $\alpha = V_g/V_\ell$:

$$\beta_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1 + \alpha}{\dfrac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}} + \dfrac{\alpha}{n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}}} = \beta_{\mathrm{oil}}\,\frac{1+\alpha}{1 + \alpha\,\dfrac{\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}}{n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}}}. \tag{7.3}$$

The gas volume itself shrinks with pressure. If the air makes up a fraction $\alpha_0$ of the total volume at atmospheric pressure $p_0$, then $V_g/V_\ell = \alpha_0/(1-\alpha_0)$ there, and the polytropic law gives

$$\alpha(p) = \frac{\alpha_0}{1-\alpha_0}\left(\frac{p_0}{p_{\mathrm{abs}}}\right)^{1/n}. \tag{7.4}$$

8 Flow through a sharp-edged orifice

Squeeze a garden hose with your thumb and the water shoots out fast: pressure turned into speed. In the valve, the speed is then lost in a turbulent swirl, and the pressure difference becomes heat.

Upstream of the opening the oil is almost still at pressure $p_u$. It accelerates through the gap and the jet keeps contracting a little beyond the edge, to its narrowest section, the vena contracta, where the streamlines are parallel and the pressure equals the downstream pressure $p_d$. Apply steady Bernoulli, (5.2) without the time term, between a point upstream and the vena contracta:

$$p_u = p_d + \tfrac12\rho\,v_j^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v_j = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(p_u - p_d)}{\rho}}.$$

The jet area is a fraction $C_c$ of the opening $A_o$ (about 0.61 for a sharp edge), and friction reduces the speed slightly. Both factors are lumped into a discharge coefficient $C_d \approx 0.6$ to $0.7$:

$$Q = C_d\,A_o\sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta p}{\rho}}, \qquad \Delta p = p_u - p_d. \tag{8.1}$$

In a spool valve the opening is a slot: $A_o = w\,x$, with $w$ the circumferential width of the port (the area gradient) and $x$ the spool travel. Normalize the travel to $\hat{x} = x/x_{\max} \in [-1, 1]$ and collect the constants into one valve coefficient:

$$Q = K_v\,\hat{x}\,\sqrt{\Delta p}, \qquad K_v = C_d\,w\,x_{\max}\sqrt{2/\rho}. \tag{8.2}$$

Manufacturers quote a rated flow $Q_N$ at a rated drop $\Delta p_N$ across the whole valve, which is two metering edges in series, each taking $\Delta p_N/2$. Hence $K_v = Q_N/\sqrt{\Delta p_N/2}$.

In our press
$Q_N = 100\ \mathrm{L/min}$ at 7 MPa gives $K_v = 8.91\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\sqrt{Pa})}$. With $C_d = 0.62$ that is a full opening $w\,x_{\max} = 30\ \mathrm{mm^2}$: a 10 mm diameter port ring opened by about 1 mm.

8.1 Taming the square root

The square root has an infinite slope at $\Delta p = 0$, which a linear analysis and a numerical integrator both dislike. Real orifices also become laminar at tiny flows, where $Q \propto \Delta p$. The model uses

$$g(\Delta p) = \frac{\Delta p}{\left(\Delta p^2 + p_{\mathrm{tr}}^2\right)^{1/4}} \approx \begin{cases} \Delta p/\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{tr}}}, & |\Delta p| \ll p_{\mathrm{tr}}\\[4pt] \operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)\sqrt{|\Delta p|}, & |\Delta p| \gg p_{\mathrm{tr}}\end{cases} \tag{8.3}$$

with $p_{\mathrm{tr}} = 0.1\ \mathrm{MPa}$. It is linear near zero, square-root far away, and smooth everywhere. It also handles flow in the reverse direction through the sign of $\Delta p$.

8.2 Leakage

Between the lands and the bore there is a radial clearance of a few micrometres. Flow through such a gap is laminar, so it is proportional to the pressure difference (chapter 11 derives this for a pipe): $Q_L = K_L\,\Delta p$, with $K_L = 2\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)}$.

9 The four-way valve

A four-way valve has four metering edges. For $x_v > 0$ the edges P→A and B→T open; for $x_v < 0$ the edges P→B and A→T open. The valve is critically lapped: at $x_v = 0$ all four edges are exactly closed and only leakage flows. Writing $x^+ = \max(\hat{x}, 0)$ and $x^- = \min(\hat{x}, 0)$, the flows out of the ports are

$$\begin{aligned} Q_A &= K_v\left[x^+\,g(p_s - p_a) + x^-\,g(p_a - p_T)\right] + K_L\left(p_s - 2p_a + p_T\right)\\ Q_B &= -K_v\left[x^+\,g(p_B - p_T) + x^-\,g(p_s - p_B)\right] + K_L\left(p_s - 2p_B + p_T\right)\\ Q_S &= K_v\left[x^+\,g(p_s - p_a) - x^-\,g(p_s - p_B)\right] + K_L\left(2p_s - p_a - p_B\right). \end{aligned} \tag{9.1}$$

Each line follows from (8.2) and (8.3) applied to the open edges, plus leakage from the pump into each port and from each port to the tank.

9.1 Where the power goes

The pump delivers hydraulic power $p_s Q_S$. The ports deliver $p_a Q_A + p_B Q_B$ to the actuator, and the tank receives $p_T(Q_S - Q_A - Q_B)$. The difference is lost in the jets as heat:

$$P_{\mathrm{valve}} = p_s Q_S - p_a Q_A - p_B Q_B - p_T\left(Q_S - Q_A - Q_B\right). \tag{9.2}$$
In our press
Over one cycle the pump does 26.3 kJ of work and the valve burns 24.4 kJ of it: 93 %. A valve throttling a constant-pressure supply is a brake. Industrial presses use variable pumps or accumulators to waste less.

9.2 The two valve coefficients

For small changes around a steady operating point, expand $Q_A(x_v, p_a)$ to first order:

$$\delta Q_A = K_q\,\delta x_v - K_c\,\delta p_a, \qquad K_q = \frac{\partial Q_A}{\partial x_v}, \qquad K_c = -\frac{\partial Q_A}{\partial p_a}. \tag{9.3}$$

$K_q$ is the flow gain and $K_c$ the flow-pressure coefficient. For $x_v > 0$ and $|\Delta p| \gg p_{\mathrm{tr}}$, (9.1) gives $K_q = K_v\sqrt{p_s - p_a}$ and $K_c = K_v x_v/(2\sqrt{p_s - p_a}) + 2K_L$. The term $K_c$ acts like a resistor that bleeds flow when the pressure rises; it supplies most of the damping of an open valve.

In our press
Cruising up at 0.1 m/s, the spool is 40.6 % open with $p_s - p_a = 9.76\ \mathrm{MPa}$: $K_q = 2.8\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$ per unit spool and $K_c = 5.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)}$, about 145 times the leakage.

10 The spool and its torque motor

A current in the torque motor tilts an armature; in a two-stage valve the armature steers a small pilot flow that pushes the main spool. The details are a chapter of their own, but measured responses of good servo valves are described well by a second-order system from the command $u$ to the spool position:

$$\ddot{x}_v + 2\zeta_v\omega_v\,\dot{x}_v + \omega_v^2\,x_v = \omega_v^2\,u, \qquad \omega_v = 2\pi f_v. \tag{10.1}$$

Its frequency response, from $u = \hat{u}\,e^{j\omega t}$, is

$$\frac{x_v}{u}(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 - (\omega/\omega_v)^2 + 2j\zeta_v\,\omega/\omega_v}, \qquad \varphi(\omega) = -\arctan\frac{2\zeta_v\,\omega/\omega_v}{1 - (\omega/\omega_v)^2}. \tag{10.2}$$
In our press
$f_v = 60\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $\zeta_v = 0.7$. At 10 Hz the spool lags by 13°, at 38 Hz already by 56°: the valve alone eats a good part of the phase a feedback loop can afford.

11 The hose: a slug of oil

11.1 Inertance

Swing the end of a garden hose full of water: the water lags behind, then pushes back. The column has mass, and a narrow bore makes it hard to get moving, because every litre per second must run fast.

Treat the oil in a hose of length $L_h$ and cross-section $A_h$ as one rigid slug of mass $\rho A_h L_h$, moving at speed $Q_h/A_h$. The pressure at the inlet pushes it forward with $p_a A_h$, the pressure at the outlet pushes it back with $p_A A_h$, and friction costs a pressure drop $\Delta p_f$. Newton's second law:

$$\rho A_h L_h\,\frac{\dot{Q}_h}{A_h} = \left(p_a - p_A - \Delta p_f\right)A_h \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I\,\dot{Q}_h = p_a - p_A - \Delta p_f, \qquad I = \frac{\rho L_h}{A_h}. \tag{11.1}$$

$I$ is the inertance, the hydraulic analogue of an inductor. It grows as the bore shrinks, $I\propto 1/d_h^2$: a narrow hose is a heavy hose.

In our press
2 m of 12 mm hose: $I = 1.54\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{kg/m^4}$. To accelerate the flow by 10 L/min in 10 ms takes $$I\,\dot{Q} = 1.54\times10^7\times\frac{1.67\times10^{-4}}{0.01} = 0.26\ \mathrm{MPa}.$$

11.2 Laminar friction: Hagen–Poiseuille

In slow, steady flow the layers of oil slide over each other. Take a cylinder of oil of radius $r$ and length $L$, coaxial with the pipe. The pressure difference pushes its ends with $\Delta p\,\pi r^2$. On its curved surface the shear stress $\tau = -\mu\,du/dr$ of the slower outer oil holds it back with $\tau\,2\pi r L$. In steady flow these balance:

$$\Delta p\,\pi r^2 = -\mu\frac{du}{dr}\,2\pi r L \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{du}{dr} = -\frac{\Delta p}{2\mu L}\,r.$$

Integrate with the no-slip condition $u(R) = 0$ at the wall:

$$u(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right).$$

Add up the flow through thin rings of area $2\pi r\,dr$:

$$Q = \int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr = \frac{\pi\Delta p}{2\mu L}\left[\frac{R^2 r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R = \frac{\pi R^4}{8\mu L}\,\Delta p.$$

With $R = d/2$:

$$\Delta p = R_{\mathrm{lam}}\,Q, \qquad R_{\mathrm{lam}} = \frac{128\,\mu L}{\pi d^4}. \tag{11.2}$$
Check
The fourth power is brutal: halve the bore and the resistance rises sixteen times. Units: $\mathrm{Pa\,s\cdot m}/\mathrm{m^4} = \mathrm{Pa\,s/m^3}$, a pressure per flow.

11.3 Turbulent friction

Whether the flow stays laminar depends on the Reynolds number $Re = \bar{u}\,d/\nu$, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. Above about 2300 the flow becomes turbulent, and the pressure drop grows with the square of the speed, as in the Darcy–Weisbach law plus the losses in fittings and bends:

$$\Delta p = \left(f\,\frac{L}{d} + K_m\right)\frac12\rho\bar{u}^2 = K_t\,Q|Q|, \qquad K_t = \frac{\rho\left(fL/d + K_m\right)}{2A^2}. \tag{11.3}$$

The model adds the two, $\Delta p_f = R_{\mathrm{lam}}Q + K_t Q|Q|$: the laminar term dominates at small flow, the turbulent term at large flow. The form $Q|Q|$ keeps the sign of the loss opposite to the flow.

In our press
At 60 L/min in the 12 mm hose the mean speed is 8.8 m/s and $Re \approx 3300$: turbulent. $R_{\mathrm{lam}} = 1.09\times10^8\ \mathrm{Pa\,s/m^3}$, $K_t = 1.64\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg/m^7}$ with $f = 0.02$ and $K_m = 1.5$.

11.4 When is one slug enough?

Pressure waves run along the hose at $c = \sqrt{\beta/\rho} \approx 1300\ \mathrm{m/s}$. A 2 m hose rings like an organ pipe at about $c/2L \approx 330\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and above. One rigid slug is a good model only well below that. The servo loop crosses over near 10 Hz, so the lumped model is good where the controller works. The 174 Hz mode it predicts, however, is already halfway to the first wave mode, so treat its exact frequency with care.

12 Chambers, ram, friction, contact

12.1 The three pressure equations

Apply the build-up equation (7.2) three times. The small volume at the valve port, $V_a$, which includes half the hose, receives $Q_A$ and sends $Q_h$ down the hose. The cap chamber receives $Q_h$ and grows as the ram rises. The rod chamber receives $Q_B$ and shrinks as the ram rises:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{V_a}{\beta(p_a)}\,\dot{p}_a &= Q_A - Q_h\\ \frac{V_A(y)}{\beta(p_A)}\,\dot{p}_A &= Q_h - A_A\dot{y}, & V_A &= V_{A0} + \tfrac12 V_h + A_A\,y\\ \frac{V_B(y)}{\beta(p_B)}\,\dot{p}_B &= Q_B + A_B\dot{y}, & V_B &= V_{B0} + A_B\,(S - y) \end{aligned} \tag{12.1}$$

The cap area is the full bore, $A_A = \pi D^2/4$; the rod side has the annulus $A_B = \pi(D^2 - d_r^2)/4$. Each chamber carries its own $\beta(p)$ from (7.3), because each sits at its own pressure.

12.2 Newton for the ram

$$m\,\ddot{y} = p_A A_A - p_B A_B - m g - F_f(\dot{y}) - F_w(y,\dot{y}) + F_{\mathrm{stop}}(y,\dot{y}). \tag{12.2}$$

The cap pressure pushes up on the full bore, the rod pressure pushes down on the annulus, gravity pulls down, and three contact forces remain.

12.3 Seal friction

Push a heavy box across a floor: it refuses to move until you push hard enough, jerks loose, then slides with less effort. Piston seals do the same.

The model combines three effects. A Coulomb force $F_c$ opposes sliding; at low speeds a higher break-away force $k_{\mathrm{st}}F_c$ (the Stribeck effect) has to be overcome; and a viscous term $b_v\dot{y}$ grows with speed. The jump of the Coulomb force at $\dot{y} = 0$ is smoothed with a hyperbolic tangent over a speed $v_s = 1\ \mathrm{mm/s}$:

$$F_f(\dot{y}) = F_c\tanh\!\left(\frac{\dot{y}}{v_s}\right)\left[1 + (k_{\mathrm{st}} - 1)\,e^{-(\dot{y}/v_{\mathrm{str}})^2}\right] + b_v\,\dot{y}. \tag{12.3}$$

The smoothing makes the equations integrable with a fixed step. Its price is that at rest the friction behaves like an enormous damper of slope $F_c k_{\mathrm{st}}/v_s \approx 10^6\ \mathrm{N\,s/m}$; problem 4 on the press page shows how that misleads a linear analysis.

12.4 The specimen and the stop

The specimen is a spring $k_w$ with a damper $c_w$ in parallel (a Kelvin–Voigt solid). It is only touched when the platen has climbed past $y_c$, and it can push but not pull:

$$F_w = \begin{cases}\max\left(0,\ k_w\,\delta + c_w\,\dot{\delta}\right), & \delta = y - y_c > 0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \tag{12.4}$$

The bottom stop is the same law with a very stiff spring, $k_s = 2\times10^8\ \mathrm{N/m}$, acting when $y < 0$.

13 The complete model

Collect the state: spool position and speed, the three pressures, the hose flow, ram position and speed,

$$\mathbf{x} = \left(x_v,\ \dot{x}_v,\ p_a,\ Q_h,\ p_A,\ p_B,\ y,\ \dot{y}\right). \tag{13.1}$$

Equations (10.1), (12.1), (11.1) and (12.2), with the valve flows (9.1), friction (12.3) and contact (12.4), form a system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, u)$ of eight first-order nonlinear differential equations driven by one input, the command $u$. That is all the servo press is. The page integrates it with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 100 steps per controller sample, and integrates seven more quantities alongside to keep the energy books (chapter 19).
