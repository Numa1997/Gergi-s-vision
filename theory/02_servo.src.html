7 Oil is a stiff spring

Part I treated oil as incompressible, and for a hand press that is fine. A servo press is different: its oil is squeezed by 12 MPa and asked to respond within milliseconds, and at that scale the oil’s small give becomes a spring that the whole machine bounces on. This chapter finds the law of that spring. It is the law of every chamber on the map of chapter 0.

7.1 The bulk modulus

Seal a syringe full of oil and lean on the plunger with all your weight: it moves by a hair. The oil is not incompressible, only very stiff.

The bulk modulus measures how much pressure it takes to squeeze a volume $V$ by a fraction $dV/V$:

$$\beta = -V\,\frac{dp}{dV} = \rho\,\frac{dp}{d\rho}. \tag{7.1}$$

The minus sign makes $\beta$ positive, since volume falls as pressure rises. The second form follows because the mass $\rho V$ of a sealed sample is fixed: $d(\rho V) = V\,d\rho + \rho\,dV = 0$, so $d\rho/\rho = -dV/V$. Mineral hydraulic oil has $\beta \approx 1.5\ \mathrm{GPa}$: squeezing it by 1 % takes 15 MPa.

Why so stiff? In a liquid the molecules already touch; squeezing it means pushing electron clouds into each other, and they resist hard. Water, at 2.2 GPa, is stiffer still. A gas is the opposite: its molecules are far apart and the pressure comes only from their impacts. For an ideal gas compressed as $pV^n = \text{const}$, differentiating gives $V^n\,dp + n p V^{n-1}dV = 0$, so

$$\beta_{\mathrm{gas}} = -V\frac{dp}{dV} = n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}.$$

The exponent $n$ is 1 for a slow, isothermal squeeze, where heat has time to leave, and $\gamma = 1.4$ for a fast, adiabatic one. Air at atmospheric pressure has $\beta_{\mathrm{gas}} = 0.10$ to $0.14\ \mathrm{MPa}$, ten thousand times softer than oil. That contrast is the whole story of section 7.3.

7.2 The pressure build-up equation

This is the most important equation in hydraulics. Take a chamber whose volume $V(t)$ can change because a piston of area $A$ moves out at speed $\dot{y}$, so $\dot{V} = A\dot{y}$. Oil flows in at $Q_{\mathrm{in}}$ and out at $Q_{\mathrm{out}}$ (Figure 2). The mass inside is $m = \rho V$. Mass is conserved, so it changes only through the flows:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho V\right) = \rho\,Q_{\mathrm{in}} - \rho\,Q_{\mathrm{out}}.$$

Expand the left side with the product rule, and use (7.1) to replace $\dot{\rho}$ by $(\rho/\beta)\dot{p}$:

$$\rho\dot{V} + V\dot{\rho} = \rho\dot{V} + \frac{\rho V}{\beta}\dot{p} = \rho\left(Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}}\right).$$

Divide by $\rho$:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{V}{\beta}\,\dot{p} = Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}} - \dot{V}\;} \tag{7.2}$$

Read it as a budget: the net flow into the chamber either moves the boundary ($\dot V$) or compresses the oil already there ($V\dot p/\beta$). The ratio $C = V/\beta$ is the hydraulic capacitance; it plays the role of a capacitor, with pressure as voltage and flow as current. Every chamber of the servo press obeys (7.2).

Check
Units: $\mathrm{m^3/Pa}\cdot\mathrm{Pa/s} = \mathrm{m^3/s}$. Limit $\beta\to\infty$: the left side vanishes and $Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}} = \dot{V}$, the incompressible continuity of chapter 2.

7.3 Air in the oil

A few bubbles turn the stiff spring into a soft one. Bubbles are springs that stiffen as you squeeze them: crush them to a fortieth of their size and they barely matter.

Take a mixture of a liquid volume $V_\ell$ and a gas volume $V_g$. When the pressure rises by $dp$ both shrink. The liquid follows (7.1): $dV_\ell = -V_\ell\,dp/\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}$. The gas follows a polytropic law $p_{\mathrm{abs}}V_g^n = \text{const}$, whose differential is $n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}V_g^{n-1}dV_g + V_g^n\,dp = 0$, so $dV_g = -V_g\,dp/(n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}})$. The compliances add:

$$\frac{dV}{dp} = -\frac{V_\ell}{\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}} - \frac{V_g}{n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}}.$$

Apply the definition (7.1) to the mixture, $\beta_{\mathrm{eff}} = -(V_\ell + V_g)/(dV/dp)$, and divide top and bottom by $V_\ell$, writing $\alpha = V_g/V_\ell$:

$$\beta_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1 + \alpha}{\dfrac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}} + \dfrac{\alpha}{n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}}} = \beta_{\mathrm{oil}}\,\frac{1+\alpha}{1 + \alpha\,\dfrac{\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}}{n\,p_{\mathrm{abs}}}}. \tag{7.3}$$

The gas volume itself shrinks with pressure, while the liquid volume, used as the reference in $\alpha = V_g/V_\ell$, is taken at its unsqueezed value: at 12 MPa the oil itself shrinks by only $p/\beta_{\mathrm{oil}} \approx 0.8\,\%$, and that compression is already counted in (7.3) through its $1/\beta_{\mathrm{oil}}$ term. If the air makes up a fraction $\alpha_0$ of the total volume at atmospheric pressure $p_0$, its volume there is $V_{g0}$ with $V_{g0}/(V_\ell + V_{g0}) = \alpha_0$, so $V_{g0}/V_\ell = \alpha_0/(1-\alpha_0)$. The polytropic law carries the bubble from $p_0$ to $p_{\mathrm{abs}}$, $p_{\mathrm{abs}}V_g^n = p_0 V_{g0}^n$, so $V_g = V_{g0}\,(p_0/p_{\mathrm{abs}})^{1/n}$. Divide by $V_\ell$:

$$\alpha(p) = \frac{\alpha_0}{1-\alpha_0}\left(\frac{p_0}{p_{\mathrm{abs}}}\right)^{1/n}. \tag{7.4}$$

8 Flow through a sharp-edged orifice

Every edge of the valve is an orifice: a sharp-edged opening that the spool makes larger or smaller. This chapter derives how much oil flows through one, given the pressures on its two sides. It is the law of the “valve edges” box on the map.

giventhe opening $A_o$ and the pressures $p_u$ upstream and $p_d$ downstream
a sharp-edged orificenonlinear resistor
answers withthe flow $Q$
Squeeze a garden hose with your thumb and the water shoots out fast: pressure turned into speed. In the valve, the speed is then lost in a turbulent swirl, and the pressure difference becomes heat.

Upstream of the opening the oil is almost still at pressure $p_u$. It accelerates through the gap and the jet keeps contracting a little beyond the edge, to its narrowest section, the vena contracta, where the streamlines are parallel and the pressure equals the downstream pressure $p_d$. Apply steady Bernoulli, (5.2) without the time term, between a point upstream and the vena contracta:

$$p_u = p_d + \tfrac12\rho\,v_j^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v_j = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(p_u - p_d)}{\rho}}.$$

The flow through the opening is the jet speed times the jet’s area at the vena contracta, and that area is smaller than the hole: a fraction $C_c$ of the opening $A_o$, the contraction coefficient. Why the jet contracts at all, and by how much, is the subject of the next section. A little friction also slows the jet, by a factor $C_v \approx 0.98$. Both factors are lumped into a discharge coefficient $C_d = C_cC_v \approx 0.6$ to $0.7$:

$$Q = C_d\,A_o\sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta p}{\rho}}, \qquad \Delta p = p_u - p_d. \tag{8.1}$$

In a spool valve a stepped rod, the spool, slides in a close-fitting bore. Its wide sections, the lands, cover and uncover slots cut in the wall of the bore. The opening is such a slot: $A_o = w\,x$, with $w$ the circumferential width of the port (the area gradient) and $x$ the spool travel. Normalize the travel to $\hat{x} = x/x_{\max} \in [-1, 1]$ and collect the constants into one valve coefficient:

$$Q = K_v\,\hat{x}\,\sqrt{\Delta p}, \qquad K_v = C_d\,w\,x_{\max}\sqrt{2/\rho}. \tag{8.2}$$

Manufacturers quote a rated flow $Q_N$ at a rated drop $\Delta p_N$ across the whole valve, which is two metering edges in series, each taking $\Delta p_N/2$. Hence $K_v = Q_N/\sqrt{\Delta p_N/2}$.

In our press
$Q_N = 100\ \mathrm{L/min}$ at 7 MPa gives $K_v = 8.91\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\sqrt{Pa})}$. With $C_d = 0.62$ that is a full opening $w\,x_{\max} = 30\ \mathrm{mm^2}$: a 10 mm diameter port ring opened by about 1 mm.

8.1 Why the jet is narrower than the hole

Watch a bath drain or a tap fill a glass: the stream narrows just after it leaves the opening. Oil arriving from the sides cannot turn the corner at the edge instantly; it keeps some sideways speed and squeezes the jet.

One case can be solved exactly from momentum alone. Let a tank drain through a short tube that pokes into the tank, a “Borda mouthpiece”. Far from the tube’s mouth the oil is almost still, so the pressure on every part of the tank wall is the hydrostatic value, with one exception: the patch of wall opposite the mouth, of area $A_o$, has no wall facing it on the other side. Take the oil in the tank as a control volume and balance momentum along the tube axis. The only unbalanced force is the pressure excess $(p_u - p_d)$ on that missing patch, and it must supply the momentum carried out by the jet each second:

$$(p_u - p_d)\,A_o = \rho\,Q\,v_j = \rho\,(C_cA_o\,v_j)\,v_j.$$

Bernoulli along a streamline into the jet gives $p_u - p_d = \tfrac12\rho v_j^2$. Substitute:

$$\tfrac12\rho v_j^2\,A_o = \rho\,C_cA_o\,v_j^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad C_c = \tfrac12.$$

The jet has half the area of the hole, exactly. Momentum demands it: Bernoulli turns the pressure difference into a speed, but the same pressure difference acting on the hole can only supply half the momentum that a full-bore jet at that speed would carry.

For a sharp edge in a flat wall, the wall next to the edge does feel the moving oil, so the momentum balance no longer closes on its own. The free-streamline theory of Helmholtz and Kirchhoff solves that case with conformal mapping, and von Mises worked it out for many shapes in 1917; the derivation is beyond this text, so take the result as cited. For a long two-dimensional slot, the case of a valve edge, it gives

$$C_c = \frac{\pi}{\pi + 2} = 0.611.$$

That is the 0.61 measured in practice. The two cases bracket reality: a re-entrant tube contracts the jet most ($C_c = 0.5$), a sharp edge in a flat wall less (0.611), a rounded entry hardly at all (close to 1). A valve edge, with oil arriving along the spool rather than head-on, behaves much the same, which is why $C_d \approx 0.6$ to $0.7$ is the working value.

8.2 Taming the square root

The square root has an infinite slope at $\Delta p = 0$, which a linear analysis and a numerical integrator both dislike. Real orifices also become laminar at tiny flows, where $Q \propto \Delta p$. The model uses

$$g(\Delta p) = \frac{\Delta p}{\left(\Delta p^2 + p_{\mathrm{tr}}^2\right)^{1/4}} \quad \approx \begin{cases} \Delta p/\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{tr}}}, & |\Delta p| \ll p_{\mathrm{tr}}\\[4pt] \operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)\sqrt{|\Delta p|}, & |\Delta p| \gg p_{\mathrm{tr}}\end{cases} \tag{8.3}$$

with $p_{\mathrm{tr}} = 0.1\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Both limits follow from the denominator. When $|\Delta p| \ll p_{\mathrm{tr}}$, $(\Delta p^2 + p_{\mathrm{tr}}^2)^{1/4} \to p_{\mathrm{tr}}^{1/2}$, so $g \to \Delta p/\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{tr}}}$: a straight line, like laminar flow. When $|\Delta p| \gg p_{\mathrm{tr}}$, $(\Delta p^2)^{1/4} = |\Delta p|^{1/2}$, so $g \to \Delta p/\sqrt{|\Delta p|} = \operatorname{sign}(\Delta p)\sqrt{|\Delta p|}$. At $\Delta p = p_{\mathrm{tr}}$ the two meet, and $g$ is $2^{-1/4} = 0.84$ of the pure square root. The function is smooth everywhere and handles reverse flow through the sign of $\Delta p$. It is a modelling choice, not a law of nature: it buys a well-behaved equation at the price of a small error in a range of pressure differences the press passes through quickly.

8.3 Leakage

Even a closed edge leaks. Between each land and the bore there is a radial clearance $c$ of micrometres, and oil creeps through it along the land, a length $L$, from the high-pressure side to the low. The gap is so thin that the flow is laminar. Unroll the ring into a flat slit of width $w = \pi d$ for a spool of diameter $d$. Take a thin layer of oil between heights $z$ and $z + dz$ across the gap. The pressure difference pushes it along with $\Delta p\,w\,dz$; the shear stress $\tau = \eta\,du/dz$ of its neighbours acts on its top and bottom faces, of area $wL$. In steady flow the forces balance:

$$\Delta p\,w\,dz + \left[\tau(z + dz) - \tau(z)\right]wL = 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \eta\,\frac{d^2u}{dz^2} = -\frac{\Delta p}{L}.$$

Integrate twice with the oil at rest on both walls, $u(0) = u(c) = 0$, and add up the flow across the gap:

$$u(z) = \frac{\Delta p}{2\eta L}\,z\,(c - z), \qquad Q_L = w\int_0^c u\,dz = \frac{w\,c^3}{12\,\eta L}\,\Delta p = K_L\,\Delta p.$$

The flow is proportional to the pressure difference, and to the cube of the clearance. The model uses $K_L = 2\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)}$ for each leakage path. On a 10 mm spool with 1 mm of land and $\eta = 27.8\ \mathrm{mPa\,s}$, that is a radial clearance of 13 µm; halve the clearance and the leakage falls eightfold, which is why servo spools are lapped into their bores.

9 The four-way valve

Put four orifices of chapter 8 into one bore, all opened and closed by one spool, and you have the valve that steers the press. This chapter writes down the flow through each of its edges, adds them into the flows at its ports, and finds the two numbers, $K_q$ and $K_c$, that summarise how the valve answers small changes.

From here on $x_v$ is the normalized spool travel $\hat{x}$ of (8.2), between −1 and 1. The pump holds the supply pressure $p_s$ at port P, and the tank pressure $p_T$ (zero here) sits at port T. Port A feeds the long hose, and its pressure is $p_a$; port B feeds the rod chamber through a short rigid line, so its pressure is the rod-chamber pressure $p_B$. A four-way valve has four metering edges. For $x_v > 0$ the edges P→A and B→T open; for $x_v < 0$ the edges P→B and A→T open. The valve is critically lapped: each land is exactly as wide as the slot it covers, with neither overlap nor gap, so at $x_v = 0$ all four edges are exactly closed and only leakage flows. Writing $x^+ = \max(\hat{x}, 0)$ and $x^- = \min(\hat{x}, 0)$, the flows out of the ports are

$$\begin{aligned} Q_A &= K_v\left[x^+\,g(p_s - p_a) + x^-\,g(p_a - p_T)\right]\\ &\quad + K_L\left(p_s - 2p_a + p_T\right),\\ Q_B &= -K_v\left[x^+\,g(p_B - p_T) + x^-\,g(p_s - p_B)\right]\\ &\quad + K_L\left(p_s - 2p_B + p_T\right),\\ Q_S &= K_v\left[x^+\,g(p_s - p_a) - x^-\,g(p_s - p_B)\right]\\ &\quad + K_L\left(2p_s - p_a - p_B\right). \end{aligned} \tag{9.1}$$

9.1 Where the power goes

The pump delivers hydraulic power $p_s Q_S$. The ports deliver $p_a Q_A + p_B Q_B$ to the actuator, and the tank receives $p_T(Q_S - Q_A - Q_B)$. The difference is lost in the jets as heat:

$$\begin{aligned} P_{\mathrm{valve}} &= p_s Q_S - p_a Q_A - p_B Q_B\\ &\quad - p_T\left(Q_S - Q_A - Q_B\right). \end{aligned} \tag{9.2}$$
In our press
Over one cycle the pump does 26.3 kJ of work and the valve burns 24.4 kJ of it: 93 %. A valve throttling a constant-pressure supply is a brake. Industrial presses use variable pumps or accumulators to waste less.

9.2 The two valve coefficients

For small changes around a steady operating point, expand $Q_A(x_v, p_a)$ to first order:

$$\delta Q_A = K_q\,\delta x_v - K_c\,\delta p_a, \qquad K_q = \frac{\partial Q_A}{\partial x_v}, \qquad K_c = -\frac{\partial Q_A}{\partial p_a}. \tag{9.3}$$

$K_q$ is the flow gain: how much more oil the port delivers per unit of spool travel. $K_c$ is the flow-pressure coefficient: how much less it delivers per pascal of back-pressure. For $x_v > 0$ and $|\Delta p| \gg p_{\mathrm{tr}}$ only the P→A edge and the leaks matter, $Q_A = K_v x_v\sqrt{p_s - p_a} + K_L(p_s - 2p_a + p_T)$, and differentiating gives

$$K_q = K_v\sqrt{p_s - p_a}, \qquad K_c = K_v x_v\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{p_s - p_a}} + 2K_L.$$

The term $K_c$ acts like a resistor that bleeds flow when the pressure rises; it supplies most of the damping of an open valve.

In our press
Cruising up at 0.1 m/s, the spool is 40.6 % open with $p_s - p_a = 9.75\ \mathrm{MPa}$: $K_q = 2.8\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$ per unit spool and $K_c = 5.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3/(s\,Pa)}$, about 290 times the leakage conductance $K_L$.

10 The spool and its torque motor

The controller’s command is a small electric current. Something has to turn it into a spool position, and that something has inertia and springs, so it cannot follow the command instantly. This chapter derives how it lags.

10.1 Newton for the spool

Hang a weight from a spring in a jar of honey and tug the top of the spring: the weight follows, a moment late and without bouncing much. The spool is that weight.

Take the simplest servo valve, a single-stage one, in which the torque motor pushes the spool directly. The coil current $i$ produces a force $K_{\mathrm{coil}}\,i$ on the spool. Centring springs of stiffness $k$ pull it back to the middle, oil films and the motor’s eddy currents damp it with a force $b_s\,\dot{x}$, and the spool has mass $m_s$. Newton:

$$m_s\,\ddot{x} = K_{\mathrm{coil}}\,i - k\,x - b_s\,\dot{x}.$$

Divide by $m_s$ and name the two combinations that appear: the undamped natural frequency $\omega_v = \sqrt{k/m_s}$ and the damping ratio $\zeta_v = b_s/(2\sqrt{k m_s})$, so that $k/m_s = \omega_v^2$ and $b_s/m_s = 2\zeta_v\omega_v$. Measure the travel as a fraction of full stroke, $x_v = x/x_{\max}$, and the command as a fraction of the current that holds the spool at full stroke, $u = K_{\mathrm{coil}} i/(k\,x_{\max})$. Then

$$\ddot{x}_v + 2\zeta_v\omega_v\,\dot{x}_v + \omega_v^2\,x_v = \omega_v^2\,u, \qquad \omega_v = 2\pi f_v. \tag{10.1}$$

A steady command gives $x_v = u$: the spool goes where it is told, once it has had time. In a two-stage valve the torque motor moves a flapper that steers a small pilot flow, and the pilot flow pushes the main spool; there are more parts and more equations, but measured responses of good two-stage valves are close to (10.1) over the band where they are used, so manufacturers quote $f_v$ and $\zeta_v$ and the model uses the same form.

10.2 How the spool lags a sine

Command a sine, $u = \hat{u}\,e^{j\omega t}$. Once the start-up transient has died away the spool moves at the same frequency, $x_v = \hat{x}\,e^{j\omega t}$, so each time derivative brings down a factor $j\omega$. Substitute into (10.1): $(-\omega^2 + 2j\zeta_v\omega_v\omega + \omega_v^2)\,\hat{x} = \omega_v^2\,\hat{u}$. Divide by $\omega_v^2\hat{u}$ to get the ratio of output to input, a complex number whose size is the gain and whose angle is the phase:

$$\frac{x_v}{u}(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 - (\omega/\omega_v)^2 + 2j\zeta_v\,\omega/\omega_v}, \qquad \varphi(\omega) = -\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(2\zeta_v\,\frac{\omega}{\omega_v},\ 1 - \frac{\omega^2}{\omega_v^2}\right). \tag{10.2}$$

At $\omega = \omega_v$ the real part of the denominator vanishes and the response is $1/(2j\zeta_v)$: the spool lags by exactly 90°, whatever the damping. That is how $f_v$ is measured. Here $\operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$ is the angle of the complex number $a + jb$, so the phase runs smoothly from 0 through −90° at $\omega_v$ towards −180°. The plain form $\varphi = -\arctan(b/a)$ would be wrong above resonance, where $a < 0$: at 100 Hz it gives +52.7° instead of −127.3°.

In our press
$f_v = 60\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $\zeta_v = 0.7$. At 10 Hz the spool lags by 13°, at 38 Hz already by 56°: the valve alone eats a good part of the phase a feedback loop can afford.

11 The hose: a slug of oil

Two metres of hose join the valve to the cylinder. The oil in it weighs only 0.2 kg, but it has to be pushed back and forth every time the valve changes its mind, and through a 12 mm bore that takes a surprising pressure. This chapter finds that pressure, the friction on the way, and the limit beyond which a hose stops behaving like one lump of oil.

11.1 Inertance

Swing the end of a garden hose full of water: the water lags behind, then pushes back. The column has mass, and a narrow bore makes it hard to get moving, because every litre per second must run fast.

Treat the oil in a hose of length $L_h$ and cross-section $A_h$ as one rigid slug of mass $\rho A_h L_h$, moving at speed $Q_h/A_h$. The pressure at the inlet pushes it forward with $p_a A_h$, the pressure at the outlet pushes it back with $p_A A_h$, and friction costs a pressure drop $\Delta p_f$. Newton's second law:

$$\rho A_h L_h\,\frac{\dot{Q}_h}{A_h} = \left(p_a - p_A - \Delta p_f\right)A_h \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I\,\dot{Q}_h = p_a - p_A - \Delta p_f, \qquad I = \frac{\rho L_h}{A_h}. \tag{11.1}$$

$I$ is the inertance, the hydraulic analogue of an inductor. It grows as the bore shrinks, $I\propto 1/d_h^2$: a narrow hose is a heavy hose.

In our press
2 m of 12 mm hose: $I = 1.54\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{kg/m^4}$. To accelerate the flow by 10 L/min in 10 ms takes $$I\,\dot{Q} = 1.54\times10^7\times\frac{1.67\times10^{-4}}{0.01} = 0.26\ \mathrm{MPa}.$$

11.2 Laminar friction: Hagen–Poiseuille

In slow, steady flow the layers of oil slide over each other. Take a cylinder of oil of radius $r$ and length $L$, coaxial with the pipe. The pressure difference pushes its ends with $\Delta p\,\pi r^2$. On its curved surface the shear stress $\tau = -\eta\,du/dr$ of the slower outer oil holds it back, where $\eta = \rho\nu$ is the dynamic viscosity (written $\eta$ here because $\mu$ is already the effective mass of Part I) with $\tau\,2\pi r L$. In steady flow these balance:

$$\Delta p\,\pi r^2 = -\eta\frac{du}{dr}\,2\pi r L \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{du}{dr} = -\frac{\Delta p}{2\eta L}\,r.$$

Integrate with the no-slip condition $u(R) = 0$ at the wall:

$$u(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\eta L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right).$$

Add up the flow through thin rings of area $2\pi r\,dr$:

$$\begin{aligned} Q &= \int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,dr = \frac{\pi\Delta p}{2\eta L}\left[\frac{R^2 r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^R\\ &= \frac{\pi R^4}{8\eta L}\,\Delta p. \end{aligned}$$

With $R = d/2$:

$$\Delta p = R_{\mathrm{lam}}\,Q, \qquad R_{\mathrm{lam}} = \frac{128\,\eta L}{\pi d^4}. \tag{11.2}$$
Check
The fourth power is brutal: halve the bore and the resistance rises sixteen times. Units: $\mathrm{Pa\,s\cdot m}/\mathrm{m^4} = \mathrm{Pa\,s/m^3}$, a pressure per flow.

11.3 Turbulent friction

Whether the flow stays laminar depends on the Reynolds number $Re = \bar{u}\,d/\nu$, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. Above about 2300 the flow becomes turbulent. Eddies then carry momentum across the pipe far more effectively than viscosity does, and the wall stress no longer depends on the viscosity but on the density and the speed. Dimensional analysis then fixes the form: the only pressure that can be built from $\rho$ and $\bar{u}$ is $\tfrac12\rho\bar{u}^2$, and the drop must grow in proportion to the length per diameter, $L/d$. The dimensionless factor in front, the friction factor $f$, is measured. Adding the losses in fittings and bends, $K_m$, gives the Darcy–Weisbach law:

$$\Delta p = \left(f\,\frac{L}{d} + K_m\right)\frac12\rho\bar{u}^2 = K_t\,Q|Q|, \qquad K_t = \frac{\rho\left(fL/d + K_m\right)}{2A^2}. \tag{11.3}$$

The model adds the two, $\Delta p_f = R_{\mathrm{lam}}Q + K_t Q|Q|$: the laminar term dominates at small flow, the turbulent term at large flow. The form $Q|Q|$ keeps the sign of the loss opposite to the flow.

In our press
At 60 L/min in the 12 mm hose the mean speed is 8.8 m/s and $Re \approx 3300$: turbulent. $R_{\mathrm{lam}} = 1.09\times10^8\ \mathrm{Pa\,s/m^3}$, $K_t = 1.64\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{kg/m^7}$ with $f = 0.02$ and $K_m = 1.5$. Be warned that $f = 0.02$ is on the low side: Blasius’s correlation for smooth pipes, $f = 0.316\,Re^{-1/4}$, gives 0.042 at this Reynolds number, The pipe term $fL/d$ is 3.3 with the model’s value and 7.0 with Blasius’s, against $K_m = 1.5$ for the fittings, so the model’s turbulent loss in the hose, proportional to $fL/d + K_m$, is about 40 % low (4.8 against 8.5). Hose friction is a few hundred kilopascals at full flow, small beside the megapascals burnt in the valve (chapter 9), so the conclusions do not change.

11.4 When is one slug enough?

The slug model assumes that a push at one end of the hose moves all of its oil at once. It does not: a push travels as a pressure wave. Follow a short piece of the oil column, $dx$ long, as a small pressure disturbance $p(x,t)$ and speed $u(x,t)$ pass through. Mass conservation for the piece, with (7.1), says that oil piling up raises the pressure: $\partial p/\partial t = -\beta\,\partial u/\partial x$. Newton for the piece says that a pressure gradient accelerates it: $\rho\,\partial u/\partial t = -\partial p/\partial x$. Differentiate the first in time, the second in space, and eliminate $u$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial t^2} = \frac{\beta}{\rho}\,\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2}, \qquad c = \sqrt{\beta/\rho} \approx 1300\ \mathrm{m/s}.$$

That is the wave equation, and $c$ is the speed of sound in oil. (A rubber hose bulges as a wave passes and slows it somewhat, the Korteweg correction; the model ignores that.) A 2 m hose rings like an organ pipe at about $c/2L \approx 330\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and above. One rigid slug is a good model only well below that. The servo loop crosses over near 10 Hz (its loop gain falls through 1 there; chapter 18), so the lumped model is good where the controller works. The 174 Hz mode it predicts, the slug bouncing between the port volume and the cap chamber (worked solution 2 on the Servo-Hydraulic Press page evaluates it with the volumes of (12.1)), is however already halfway to the first wave mode, so treat its exact frequency with care.

12.1 The three pressure equations

Apply the build-up equation (7.2) three times. The small volume at the valve port, $V_a$, which includes half the hose, receives $Q_A$ and sends $Q_h$ down the hose. The cap chamber receives $Q_h$ and grows as the ram rises. The rod chamber receives $Q_B$ and shrinks as the ram rises. The hose holds the volume $V_h = A_hL_h$; half of it is counted with each end. That split is the usual way to lump a line: the slug of chapter 11 carries the hose’s mass, and its compressibility, spread along its length, is shared between the two volumes it joins. Mind the case of the subscripts: $p_a$ is the pressure at the valve end of the hose, $p_A$ the pressure in the cap chamber at its far end.

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{V_a}{\beta(p_a)}\,\dot{p}_a &= Q_A - Q_h\\ \frac{V_A(y)}{\beta(p_A)}\,\dot{p}_A &= Q_h - A_A\dot{y}\\ \frac{V_B(y)}{\beta(p_B)}\,\dot{p}_B &= Q_B + A_B\dot{y}\\ V_A &= V_{A0} + \tfrac12 V_h + A_A\,y\\ V_B &= V_{B0} + A_B\,(S - y),\qquad S = 0.2\ \mathrm{m} \end{aligned} \tag{12.1}$$

The cap area is the full bore, $A_A = \pi D^2/4$; the rod side has the annulus $A_B = \pi(D^2 - d_r^2)/4$. Each chamber carries its own $\beta(p)$ from (7.3), because each sits at its own pressure.

12.2 Newton for the ram

$$\begin{aligned} m\,\ddot{y} &= p_A A_A - p_B A_B - m g\\ &\quad - F_f(\dot{y}) - F_w(y,\dot{y}) + F_{\mathrm{stop}}(y,\dot{y}). \end{aligned} \tag{12.2}$$

The cap pressure pushes up on the full bore, the rod pressure pushes down on the annulus, gravity pulls down, and three contact forces remain.

12.3 Seal friction

Push a heavy box across a floor: it refuses to move until you push hard enough, jerks loose, then slides with less effort. Piston seals do the same.

Seal friction is not derived from first principles here, because nobody can: it depends on the rubber, the oil film and the surface finish. What can be explained is its shape. In 1902 Richard Stribeck measured the friction in lubricated bearings as a function of speed and found that it falls as speed rises from zero, reaches a minimum, and then grows again (Stribeck, Zeitschrift des Vereines deutscher Ingenieure 46, 1902). At rest and at crawling speed the seal touches the rod through a broken oil film, and solid friction dominates. As speed rises, the oil is dragged into the contact and lifts the seal off, so friction falls. At higher speed the film is complete and its viscous shear grows with speed.

The model captures that curve with three terms. A Coulomb force $F_c$ opposes sliding; at low speeds a higher break-away force $k_{\mathrm{st}}F_c$ (the Stribeck effect) has to be overcome; and a viscous term $b_v\dot{y}$ grows with speed. The jump of the Coulomb force at $\dot{y} = 0$ is smoothed with a hyperbolic tangent over a speed $v_s = 1\ \mathrm{mm/s}$, and the Stribeck bump dies away over $v_{\mathrm{str}} = 5\ \mathrm{mm/s}$:

$$\begin{aligned} F_f(\dot{y}) &= F_c\tanh\!\left(\frac{\dot{y}}{v_s}\right)\left[1 + (k_{\mathrm{st}} - 1)\,e^{-(\dot{y}/v_{\mathrm{str}})^2}\right]\\ &\quad + b_v\,\dot{y}. \end{aligned} \tag{12.3}$$

The smoothing makes the equations integrable with a fixed step. Its price is that at rest the friction behaves like an enormous damper of slope $F_c k_{\mathrm{st}}/v_s \approx 10^6\ \mathrm{N\,s/m}$; worked solution 4 on the Servo-Hydraulic Press page shows how that misleads a linear analysis.

The specimen is a spring $k_w$ with a damper $c_w$ in parallel (a Kelvin–Voigt solid); the damper is set to 5 % of critical. Critical damping is the value that separates bouncing from creeping: for a mass $m$ on a spring $k$ with damper $c$, the motion $m\ddot{\delta} + c\dot{\delta} + k\delta = 0$ goes as $e^{\lambda t}$ with $m\lambda^2 + c\lambda + k = 0$, whose roots are real, so the motion does not oscillate, once $c^2 \ge 4mk$. The boundary is $c_{\mathrm{crit}} = 2\sqrt{km}$, and here $c_w = 0.05\cdot 2\sqrt{k_w m}$, with $m = 160\ \mathrm{kg}$ the ram that presses on the specimen: a lightly damped specimen, as a steel part is. It is only touched when the platen has climbed past $y_c$, and it can push but not pull:

$$F_w = \begin{cases}\max\left(0,\ k_w\,\delta + c_w\,\dot{\delta}\right), & \delta = y - y_c > 0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \tag{12.4}$$

The bottom stop is the same law with a very stiff spring, $k_s = 2\times10^8\ \mathrm{N/m}$, acting when $y < 0$.

13 The complete model

Put the blocks of the map together. What does the model have to remember from one instant to the next? Exactly one number for each form of stored energy, because energy cannot jump: the spool stores kinetic energy in its speed and spring energy in its position; each of the three volumes of oil stores compression energy in its pressure; the hose slug stores kinetic energy in its flow; the ram stores kinetic energy in its speed, and potential energy, in gravity and in the specimen, in its position. That makes eight, and they are the state:

$$\mathbf{x} = \left(x_v,\ \dot{x}_v,\ p_a,\ Q_h,\ p_A,\ p_B,\ y,\ \dot{y}\right). \tag{13.1}$$

Equations (10.1), (12.1), (11.1) and (12.2), with the valve flows (9.1), friction (12.3) and contact (12.4), form a system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, u)$ of eight first-order nonlinear differential equations driven by one input, the command $u$. Every other quantity, the valve flows, the friction, the specimen force, is computed from the state at each instant: these are the dissipating blocks, the resistors, which store nothing. That is all the servo press is. The page integrates it with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 100 steps per controller sample, and integrates seven more quantities alongside to keep the energy books (chapter 19).
