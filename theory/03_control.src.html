14 Operating points and linearization

Part II built the press as eight nonlinear equations. A computer can integrate them, and the animation does, but they cannot answer the question a designer cares about: if I raise the controller gain, will the press shake? That question has a sharp answer only for linear systems, whose responses add and scale, and whose behaviour is fixed by a handful of numbers, the poles. So we look at the press near one way of running, where small disturbances obey linear equations. This chapter finds those ways of running and the linear equations around them, and ends with the tool that makes linear systems easy: the transfer function.

14.1 Steady operation

An operating point is a state $\mathbf{x}_0$ and input $u_0$ at which nothing changes except, possibly, the ram position, which may drift at a constant speed $v_0$. Setting the right-hand sides of section 13 to zero gives three conditions: the valve delivers exactly the flow the moving ram needs on each side, $Q_A = A_A v_0$ and $Q_B = -A_B v_0$; and the forces on the ram balance,

$$p_A A_A - p_B A_B = mg + F_f(v_0) + F_w.$$

One more condition comes from the hose: in steady flow its slug does not accelerate, so (11.1) leaves $p_a - p_A = \Delta p_f(Q_h)$, with $Q_h = Q_A$. Four equations, four unknowns $(x_v, p_a, p_A, p_B)$: the page solves them with Newton's method.

In our press
Cruising up at 0.1 m/s at $y = 60$ mm: $x_v = 40.6\,\%$, $p_A = 1.91\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $p_B = 3.03\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Holding 60 kN: $x_v = 0.039\,\%$, $p_A = 7.79\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $p_B = 4.21\ \mathrm{MPa}$. At rest the critically lapped valve balances the leakages with the spool just off centre.

14.2 Small deviations

Write $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_0 + \delta\mathbf{x}$ and $u = u_0 + \delta u$, and expand $\mathbf{f}$ to first order in a Taylor series:

$$\delta\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_0, u_0) + \underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}}_{A}\,\delta\mathbf{x} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial u}}_{B}\,\delta u + O\!\left(\|\delta\mathbf{x}\|^2\right). \tag{14.1}$$

At an operating point $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_0,u_0)$ is zero, apart from the constant drift $v_0$ in the position row, which does not affect the deviations. What remains is a linear system $\delta\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\,\delta\mathbf{x} + B\,\delta u$ with an output $\delta y_{\mathrm{out}} = C\,\delta\mathbf{x}$: $C$ is a row of eight numbers. For the position loop it has a 1 in the $y$ column and zeros elsewhere; for the force loop, whose output is the specimen force $k_w\,\delta y + c_w\,\delta\dot{y}$, it has $k_w$ in the $y$ column and $c_w$ in the $\dot{y}$ column. The page computes $A$ and $B$ by central differences,

$$A_{ij} \approx \frac{f_i(\mathbf{x}_0 + h\,\mathbf{e}_j) - f_i(\mathbf{x}_0 - h\,\mathbf{e}_j)}{2h}, \tag{14.2}$$

whose error is of order $h^2$; the check script compares them with exact symbolic derivatives and finds agreement to $10^{-9}$ or better.

Each block of the map is replaced, in effect, by its tangent at the operating point: the orifice by a flow gain and a resistance (the $K_q$ and $K_c$ of chapter 9), the friction by a slope, each chamber by a capacitor with a fixed $V/\beta$. The linear model is a chain of these tangents.

Where linearization fails
The derivative must exist. The critically lapped valve has a kink at $x_v = 0$, where the flow gain jumps from $K_v\sqrt{p_a}$ to $K_v\sqrt{p_s - p_a}$, and the friction law has a near-jump at $\dot{y} = 0$. Around such points a linear model describes only one side; worked solutions 3 and 4 on the Servo-Hydraulic Press page explore what follows.

14.3 Transfer functions in one page

Ring a bell and it hums at its own notes; push it slowly and it just leans. A linear system has a small set of motions of its own, and every response is built from them.

Try a solution of the form $\delta\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{X}e^{st}$ in $\delta\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\,\delta\mathbf{x}$, with $s$ a complex number. Differentiation brings down a factor $s$, so $s\mathbf{X} = A\mathbf{X}$: the exponents $s$ the system can follow on its own are the eigenvalues of $A$. They are its poles. A pole $s = \sigma + j\omega$ is a motion that oscillates at angular frequency $\omega$ and grows or decays at rate $\sigma$. The system is stable when every pole has $\sigma < 0$: every motion of its own dies away.

To handle inputs as well, use the Laplace transform, which turns a function of time into a function of $s$,

$$X(s) = \int_0^\infty x(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt.$$

Its one essential property is what it does to a derivative. Integrate by parts:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^\infty \dot{x}\,e^{-st}\,dt &= \Big[x\,e^{-st}\Big]_0^\infty + s\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-st}\,dt\\ &= s\,X(s) - x(0). \end{aligned}$$

The bracket is $0 - x(0)$: at the upper limit $x\,e^{-st} \to 0$ for any $s$ with a large enough real part, which is the condition under which the transform exists at all.

Starting from rest, $x(0) = 0$, every time derivative becomes a multiplication by $s$. A set of linear differential equations becomes a set of algebraic ones, which can be solved for the output in terms of the input. The ratio is the transfer function: for $\delta\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\delta\mathbf{x} + B\delta u$ and $\delta y = C\delta\mathbf{x}$, $s\mathbf{X} = A\mathbf{X} + BU$ gives

$$\frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = C\left(sI - A\right)^{-1}B.$$

Its denominator is $\det(sI - A)$, which vanishes at the poles. Setting $s = j\omega$ gives the response to a sine of angular frequency $\omega$, as in chapter 10: the size of $Y/U$ is the gain, its angle the phase.

15 The classical valve-controlled cylinder

Before trusting a computer with an eight-state model, it pays to derive the textbook three-state version by hand. Take a symmetric cylinder of area $A$ (in this chapter $A$ is a piston area, not the matrix of chapter 14) with equal chamber volumes $V_t/2$, no hose, a symmetric valve, and define the load pressure $p_L = p_1 - p_2$ and the load flow $Q_L = (Q_1 + Q_2)/2$, where $Q_1$ flows into chamber 1 and $Q_2$ out of chamber 2. These two combinations are chosen because they are what the piston feels: the net force on it is $Ap_L$, and in steady motion $Q_L$ is exactly the flow that moves it. Linearized as in (9.3), the valve gives

$$Q_L = K_q\,x_v - K_c\,p_L. \tag{15.1}$$

Write the build-up equation (7.2) for each chamber, $(V_t/2\beta)\,\dot{p}_1 = Q_1 - A\dot{y} - C_{tp}p_L$ and $(V_t/2\beta)\,\dot{p}_2 = -Q_2 + A\dot{y} + C_{tp}p_L$, where $C_{tp}$ is the leakage across the piston. Subtract the second from the first and divide by 2:

$$Q_L = A\,\dot{y} + C_{tp}\,p_L + \frac{V_t}{4\beta}\,\dot{p}_L. \tag{15.2}$$

Newton for the ram of mass $m$ with viscous friction $b$ (the $b_v$ of chapter 12, written short here) and no external load: $m\ddot{y} = A\,p_L - b\,\dot{y}$. Take Laplace transforms with zero initial conditions and eliminate $Q_L$ and $p_L$. From (15.1) and (15.2), with $K_{ce} = K_c + C_{tp}$:

$$P_L(s) = \frac{K_q X_v(s) - A s Y(s)}{K_{ce} + \dfrac{V_t}{4\beta}s}.$$

Insert into $(ms^2 + bs)Y = A P_L$ and clear the fraction by multiplying both sides by $K_{ce} + V_t s/(4\beta)$:

$$\left(ms^2 + bs\right)\left(K_{ce} + \frac{V_t}{4\beta}s\right)Y = A K_q X_v - A^2 s\,Y.$$

Bring the $Y$ terms together and divide by $A^2 s$:

$$Y\left[\frac{\left(ms + b\right)\left(K_{ce} + \frac{V_t}{4\beta}s\right)}{A^2} + 1\right] = \frac{K_q}{A\,s}\,X_v.$$

Multiply out the product in the bracket:

$$\begin{aligned} &(ms + b)\left(K_{ce} + \frac{V_t s}{4\beta}\right)\\ &\qquad = \frac{V_t m}{4\beta}s^2 + \left(K_{ce}m + \frac{bV_t}{4\beta}\right)s + bK_{ce} \end{aligned}$$

and collect:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{Y(s)}{X_v(s)} &= \frac{K_q/A}{s\,N(s)},\\ N(s) &= \frac{V_t m}{4\beta A^2}s^2 + \left(\frac{K_{ce}m}{A^2} + \frac{bV_t}{4\beta A^2}\right)s\\ &\quad + 1 + \frac{bK_{ce}}{A^2}. \end{aligned} \tag{15.3}$$

The last term $bK_{ce}/A^2$ is tiny in practice: friction times leakage, both small. Dropping it, $N(s)$ is a quadratic with constant term 1. Write any such quadratic in the standard form of a damped oscillator, $s^2/\omega_h^2 + 2\zeta_h s/\omega_h + 1$, and compare coefficients. The $s^2$ terms give $1/\omega_h^2 = V_tm/(4\beta A^2)$. The $s$ terms give

$$\frac{2\zeta_h}{\omega_h} = \frac{K_{ce}m}{A^2} + \frac{bV_t}{4\beta A^2} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \zeta_h = \frac{\omega_h}{2}\left(\frac{K_{ce}m}{A^2} + \frac{bV_t}{4\beta A^2}\right),$$

and substituting $\omega_h = 2A\sqrt{\beta/(V_tm)}$ gives the $\zeta_h$ below. This is the classic form:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{Y(s)}{X_v(s)} = \frac{K_q/A}{s\left(\dfrac{s^2}{\omega_h^2} + \dfrac{2\zeta_h}{\omega_h}s + 1\right)},\qquad \omega_h^2 = \frac{4\beta A^2}{V_t\,m},\qquad \zeta_h = \frac{K_{ce}}{A}\sqrt{\frac{\beta m}{V_t}} + \frac{b}{4A}\sqrt{\frac{V_t}{\beta m}}\;} \tag{15.4}$$

Read it as two pieces. The integrator $1/s$: a valve opening produces a flow, and a flow produces a speed, not a position. And a resonance at $\omega_h$: the ram bouncing on the oil springs of its two chambers. The oil spring can be derived directly, which also handles the differential cylinder of the press, whose two chambers differ. Close both ports and push the ram up by a small $\delta y$. The cap chamber grows by $A_A\delta y$, and by (7.1) its pressure falls by $\beta_A A_A\delta y/V_A$; the force on the ram falls by $A_A$ times that. The rod chamber shrinks by $A_B\delta y$; its pressure rises by $\beta_B A_B\delta y/V_B$ and pushes down on the annulus $A_B$. Both changes oppose the push, so the two chambers are springs in parallel:

$$k_{\mathrm{oil}} = \frac{\beta_A A_A^2}{V_A} + \frac{\beta_B A_B^2}{V_B}.$$

The ram of mass $m$ bounces on them at $\omega_h^2 = k_{\mathrm{oil}}/m$:

$$\omega_h^2 = \frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{\beta_A A_A^2}{V_A} + \frac{\beta_B A_B^2}{V_B}\right). \tag{15.5}$$
In our press
With both ports closed, (15.5), with each chamber’s $\beta$ from (7.3) at its own pressure and the volumes of (12.1), gives 245 Hz near the bottom of the stroke, 213 Hz near the top and a minimum of 170 Hz at 116 mm. With the hose attached, the full model’s pole table (worked solution 2 on the Servo-Hydraulic Press page) shows the ram at 88 Hz with damping 0.26 at the cruise point: the hose slug and the extra volume soften and couple the resonance.

The shape of (15.4) explains the most famous rule of thumb in hydraulic servo design. A proportional position loop around an integrator has loop gain $K_{\mathrm{vel}}/s$, where the velocity gain $K_{\mathrm{vel}}$ (in 1/s) is the ram speed per metre of position error. It is not the valve coefficient $K_v$ of (8.2). It stays stable only if the loop gain has fallen well below 1 by the time the resonance lifts it again, which gives approximately $K_{\mathrm{vel}} < 2\zeta_h\omega_h$. Here is why. With the controller’s gain folded in, the loop gain is $L(s) = K_{\mathrm{vel}}/[s(s^2/\omega_h^2 + 2\zeta_hs/\omega_h + 1)]$. At $s = j\omega_h$ the quadratic equals $-1 + 2j\zeta_h + 1 = 2j\zeta_h$, so

$$L(j\omega_h) = \frac{K_{\mathrm{vel}}}{j\omega_h\cdot 2j\zeta_h} = -\frac{K_{\mathrm{vel}}}{2\zeta_h\omega_h}.$$

It is a negative real number: at the resonance the signal comes back exactly inverted. Chapter 18 shows that the loop goes unstable when such a signal comes back larger than it left, $|L| > 1$, which is the rule. Worked solution 9 on the Servo-Hydraulic Press page shows why the real limit is lower: the valve’s own lag and the sampling delay add phase, so the inversion happens below $\omega_h$, where the gain is larger.

16 From continuous to discrete: sample and hold

The controller is a computer. It does not see the press continuously: it reads the sensors once a millisecond, computes, and writes one number to the valve, which then stays put until the next millisecond. Two blocks of the map do this, the sampler and the hold, and this chapter finds what they do to the loop.

16.1 Solving the linear system exactly

A digital controller sees the plant only at the sampling instants $t_k = kT_s$ and holds its command constant in between (a zero-order hold). For a linear system we can then step from one sample to the next exactly. Multiply $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + B u$ by the integrating factor $e^{-At}$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-At}\mathbf{x}\right) = e^{-At}\left(\dot{\mathbf{x}} - A\mathbf{x}\right) = e^{-At}B\,u.$$

Integrate from $t_k$ to $t_{k+1} = t_k + T_s$, with $u = u_k$ constant, and substitute $\sigma = t_{k+1} - \tau$:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_{k+1} &= e^{AT_s}\mathbf{x}_k + \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} e^{A(t_{k+1}-\tau)}\,d\tau\;B\,u_k\\ &= \Phi\,\mathbf{x}_k + \Gamma\,u_k,\\ \Phi &= e^{AT_s},\qquad \Gamma = \int_0^{T_s} e^{A\sigma}\,d\sigma\,B. \end{aligned} \tag{16.1}$$

16.2 One exponential gives both

Treat the held input as an extra state that does not change: $\dot{u} = 0$. The augmented system is

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ u\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A & B\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}}_{M}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ u\end{bmatrix} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad e^{MT_s} = \begin{bmatrix}\Phi & \Gamma\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \tag{16.2}$$

Why does the corner hold $\Gamma$? Powers of the block-triangular $M$ keep its shape: $M^n = \begin{bmatrix}A^n & A^{n-1}B\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ for $n \ge 1$. Summing the exponential series term by term, the top-right block is

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{T_s^n}{n!}A^{n-1}B &= \int_0^{T_s}\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(A\sigma)^n}{n!}\,d\sigma\;B\\ &= \int_0^{T_s}e^{A\sigma}\,d\sigma\;B = \Gamma, \end{aligned}$$

exactly the integral in (16.1). The page computes $e^{MT_s}$ by scaling and squaring: it divides $MT_s$ by $2^j$ until its norm is below one half, sums the Taylor series, and squares the result $j$ times. SciPy's expm agrees to $10^{-14}$.

16.3 The z-transform

The z-transform does for sequences what the Laplace transform does for functions. Define $X(z) = \sum_{k\ge0} x_k z^{-k}$. Shifting a sequence one step ahead multiplies its transform by $z$, if it starts from zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{k\ge0} x_{k+1}z^{-k} &= z\sum_{k\ge0} x_{k+1}z^{-(k+1)}\\ &= z\left(X(z) - x_0\right) = z\,X(z). \end{aligned}$$

So $z$ means “one sample later”, and $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ becomes $z\,\mathbf{X}$. Then (16.1) gives $\mathbf{x} = (zI - \Phi)^{-1}\Gamma u$ and the discrete transfer function from command to output is

$$G(z) = C\left(zI - \Phi\right)^{-1}\Gamma. \tag{16.3}$$

A continuous pole $s$ becomes a discrete pole $z = e^{sT_s}$, because a motion $e^{st}$ seen only at $t = kT_s$ is the sequence $(e^{sT_s})^k$. The left half-plane, where continuous systems are stable, maps into the unit disc: a sampled system is stable when every pole satisfies $|z| < 1$. A sinusoid of angular frequency $\omega$ corresponds to $z = e^{j\omega T_s}$, a point on the unit circle; frequencies run up to the Nyquist frequency, $\omega = \pi/T_s$ or $f = 1/(2T_s)$, which is 500 Hz at $T_s = 1$ ms.

16.4 What the hold costs in phase

Hit the hold with one unit sample and it answers with a rectangle: height 1 for one period, then nothing. The Laplace transform of that rectangle is $\int_0^{T_s} e^{-st}\,dt = (1 - e^{-sT_s})/s$. Divided by $T_s$, so that a steady input passes with unit gain, the hold’s transfer function is $(1 - e^{-sT_s})/(sT_s)$. On the imaginary axis, factor out half a period:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1 - e^{-j\omega T_s}}{j\omega T_s} &= e^{-j\omega T_s/2}\,\frac{e^{j\omega T_s/2} - e^{-j\omega T_s/2}}{j\omega T_s}\\ &= e^{-j\omega T_s/2}\;\frac{\sin(\omega T_s/2)}{\omega T_s/2}. \end{aligned} \tag{16.4}$$

The hold delays the signal by half a sample: a phase lag of $\omega T_s/2$. The press's controller also needs one sample to compute, a further lag of $\omega T_s$. Together:

$$\varphi_{\mathrm{sampling}} = 1.5\,\omega T_s. \tag{16.5}$$
In our press
At the 9.7 Hz crossover this is 5.2° with $T_s = 1$ ms and 21.0° with $T_s = 4$ ms. The measured phase margin (the extra lag the loop could take before it oscillates; chapter 18) falls by 15.8°, almost exactly the difference.

17.1 PID in discrete time

A continuous PID controller acts on the error $e = y_{\mathrm{ref}} - y$ as $u = K_p e + K_i\int e\,dt + K_d\,\dot{e}$. Each term has a job. The proportional term pushes harder the further the ram is from where it should be. The integral term remembers: a steady load, such as the ram’s weight, needs a steady command, and a proportional term can only supply it if a steady error is left over; the integral keeps growing until that error is gone, because only then does it stop changing. The derivative term looks ahead: it brakes when the error is closing fast. In a computer the integral becomes a running sum. The backward Euler rule approximates $\int_0^{t_k} e\,dt$ by adding $T_s e_k$ each sample:

$$I_k = I_{k-1} + K_i T_s\,e_k \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{I(z)}{E(z)} = \frac{K_i T_s\,z}{z - 1}. \tag{17.1}$$

The transfer function follows from the shift rule: $I_{k-1}$ transforms to $z^{-1}I(z)$, so $I(z)(1 - z^{-1}) = K_iT_s\,E(z)$, and multiplying top and bottom by $z$ gives (17.1). Its pole at $z = 1$ is the discrete image of the integrator’s pole at $s = 0$.

A pure derivative amplifies noise without limit, so it is filtered: $D(s) = K_d\,s/(T_f s + 1)$ with $T_f = 2$ ms. Backward Euler replaces $s$ by $(1 - z^{-1})/T_s$:

$$D(z) = \frac{K_d\,\dfrac{1 - z^{-1}}{T_s}}{T_f\,\dfrac{1 - z^{-1}}{T_s} + 1} = \frac{K_d\,(z - 1)}{(T_f + T_s)\,z - T_f}. \tag{17.2}$$

Cross-multiply, $\left[(T_f + T_s)\,z - T_f\right]D(z) = K_d\,(z - 1)\,E(z)$, and read a factor $z$ as one step ahead in time: $(T_f + T_s)\,D_{k+1} - T_f\,D_k = K_d\,(e_{k+1} - e_k)$. Shift every index down by one and solve for $D_k$. In the time domain this is the line the page runs:

$$D_k = \frac{T_f}{T_f + T_s}\,D_{k-1} + \frac{K_d}{T_f + T_s}\left(e_k - e_{k-1}\right).$$

The position controller is the sum of the three parts, and the force controller is a PI:

$$K(z) = K_p + \frac{K_iT_s\,z}{z-1} + D(z), \qquad K_F(z) = K_{pF} + \frac{K_{iF}T_s\,z}{z-1}.$$

17.2 Feed-forward

Feedback corrects errors after they appear. If the controller knows the speed it is asking for, it can open the valve by the right amount in advance. From (8.2), a spool opening $\hat{x}$ against roughly half the supply pressure produces a flow $K_v\hat{x}\sqrt{p_s/2}$ and a speed $K_v\hat{x}\sqrt{p_s/2}/A_A$. Inverting:

$$u_{\mathrm{ff}} = K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,v_{\mathrm{ref}}, \qquad K_{\mathrm{ff}} = \frac{A_A}{K_v\sqrt{p_s/2}}. \tag{17.3}$$

The feedback then only has to correct what the feed-forward gets wrong.

17.3 Saturation and windup

The spool cannot open more than fully: $u$ is clipped to $[-1, 1]$. While it is clipped, the error persists and a naive integrator keeps growing; when the error finally reverses, the oversized integrator holds the valve open far too long. This is windup. The page uses conditional integration: it accepts the new integral only if the output is unsaturated, or if the error would drive it back out of saturation.

17.4 Switching laws without a bump

When the supervisor switches from position to force control, the command must not jump. At the switch the new error is zero (the force reference starts at the measured force), so $u = K_{pF}\cdot 0 + I_F$. $I_F$ is the integral state of the force law. Setting $I_F = u_{\mathrm{previous}}$ makes the command continuous. Worked solution 8 on the Servo-Hydraulic Press page removes this and measures a 2.7 kN stumble.

17.5 The supervisor

A small state machine decides which law runs: settle holds position for 0.1 s; approach follows a quintic profile, $s(\tau) = 10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5$, whose speed and acceleration are zero at both ends; creep rises at 10 mm/s until the load cell (the force sensor above the specimen) reads more than 1 kN; press ramps the force to its target and holds it; release ramps it down; retract returns on another quintic; done holds.

18 Is it stable? Closed loop, Nyquist, margins

18.1 The closed-loop matrix

Write the controller as a discrete state-space system from its input (the error) to its output (the command): $\mathbf{s}_{k+1} = A_c\mathbf{s}_k + B_c e_k$ and $u_k = C_c\mathbf{s}_k + D_c e_k$. Add one state $w_k$ for the command that is still waiting one sample to reach the valve. With $e_k = -C\mathbf{x}_k$ for deviations from the reference:

$$\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ \mathbf{s}\\ w\end{bmatrix}_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix}\Phi & 0 & \Gamma\\ -B_cC & A_c & 0\\ -D_cC & C_c & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ \mathbf{s}\\ w\end{bmatrix}_{k}. \tag{18.1}$$

Read it row by row. The plant advances by (16.1), driven by the command $w_k$ that reached the valve this sample. The controller’s memory advances on the error $-C\mathbf{x}_k$. The command it computes now, $C_c\mathbf{s}_k - D_cC\mathbf{x}_k$, is stored in $w$ and reaches the valve one sample later. The loop is stable if and only if every eigenvalue of this matrix lies inside the unit circle. The page computes them with a balanced Hessenberg QR algorithm; NumPy agrees to $10^{-15}$.

18.2 The loop gain and the Nyquist plot

Cut the loop at the controller output and follow a sinusoid around it: through the delay $z^{-1}$, the plant $G(z)$ and the controller $K(z)$. The ratio of what comes back to what went in is the loop gain

$$L(z) = K(z)\,z^{-1}\,G(z), \qquad z = e^{j\omega T_s}. \tag{18.2}$$

The closed loop amplifies by $1/(1 + L)$, so trouble comes when $L$ approaches $-1$: a signal returns inverted with the same size and reinforces itself. Nyquist's criterion makes this exact, and the reason is a fact about complex functions, the argument principle. The closed-loop poles are the values of $z$ where $1 + L(z) = 0$. Walk $z$ once around the unit circle and watch the complex number $1 + L(z)$. Each zero of $1 + L$ outside the circle, which is an unstable closed-loop pole, makes $1 + L$ wind once around the origin; each pole of $L$ outside the circle, an unstable open-loop pole, winds it once the other way. Winding $1 + L$ around 0 is the same as winding $L$ around $-1$. So plot $L(e^{j\omega T_s})$ as $\omega$ runs once around: the number of clockwise encirclements of $-1$ equals the number of unstable closed-loop poles minus the number of unstable open-loop poles. The integrators of our loops sit exactly on the unit circle, at $z = 1$, where $L$ is infinite, so the contour cannot pass through them: it steps around each on a small half-circle. Near $z = 1$, $L$ behaves like $c/(z - 1)$, and as $z$ goes round a half-circle of radius $\varepsilon$, $L$ goes round a half-circle of radius $c/\varepsilon$: that is the very large arc, and why the plotted curve comes in from infinity.

18.3 Gain and phase margins

Two numbers summarize how far the curve stays from $-1$. The phase margin is how much extra lag the loop tolerates at the crossover frequency, where $|L| = 1$: $\mathrm{PM} = 180^\circ + \angle L$. The gain margin is by how much the gain may grow at the frequency where the phase reaches $-180^\circ$: $\mathrm{GM} = -20\log_{10}|L|$ in decibels. Good designs keep $\mathrm{PM} \gtrsim 45^\circ$ and $\mathrm{GM} \gtrsim 6\ \mathrm{dB}$.

In our press
Position loop: 64.0° and 11.0 dB, crossover 9.7 Hz. Force loop: 49.5° and 10.6 dB, crossover 9.2 Hz. Raise the position gain to 90 %/mm and the closed-loop poles leave the unit circle.

19 Where the energy goes

Multiply each equation of the model by its “flow” partner and add them. The pump power $p_sQ_S$ splits as follows. The valve keeps (9.2). Each chamber turns $p\,(Q_{\mathrm{in}} - Q_{\mathrm{out}} - \dot{V})$ into compression energy; by (7.2) that is $p\,(V/\beta)\,\dot{p}$. The hose slug gains $\tfrac{d}{dt}(\tfrac12 IQ_h^2)$ and loses $\Delta p_f Q_h$ to friction. The ram gains kinetic energy $\tfrac{d}{dt}(\tfrac12 m\dot{y}^2)$ and height $mg\dot{y}$, and loses $F_f\dot{y}$ to the seals and $F_w\dot{y}$ to the specimen. Summing every term, all the internal pressures cancel in pairs. Here is the bookkeeping, with $p_T = 0$. The valve's power balance (9.2) says where the pump power goes first:

$$p_aQ_A + p_BQ_B = p_sQ_S - P_{\mathrm{valve}}.$$

Multiply the port-volume equation of (12.1) by $p_a$, the hose equation (11.1) by $Q_h$, and the two chamber equations of (12.1) by $p_A$ and $p_B$. With $I\dot{Q}_hQ_h = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 IQ_h^2\right)$:

$$\begin{aligned} p_aQ_A &= p_a\frac{V_a}{\beta}\dot{p}_a + p_aQ_h,\\ p_aQ_h &= p_AQ_h + \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 IQ_h^2\right) + \Delta p_fQ_h,\\ p_AQ_h &= p_A\frac{V_A}{\beta}\dot{p}_A + p_AA_A\dot{y},\\ p_BQ_B &= p_B\frac{V_B}{\beta}\dot{p}_B - p_BA_B\dot{y}. \end{aligned}$$

Add the four lines. The internal powers $p_aQ_h$ and $p_AQ_h$ each appear once on the left and once on the right, and cancel. Then multiply the ram equation (12.2) by $\dot{y}$, using $m\ddot{y}\dot{y} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\dot{y}^2\right)$ and $mg\dot{y} = \frac{d}{dt}(mgy)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \left(p_AA_A - p_BA_B\right)\dot{y} &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\dot{y}^2 + mgy\right)\\ &\quad + F_f\dot{y} + F_w\dot{y} - F_{\mathrm{stop}}\dot{y}. \end{aligned}$$

Substitute it for the last terms of the sum, and the sum for $p_aQ_A + p_BQ_B$ in the valve balance:

$$\begin{aligned} p_sQ_S &= P_{\mathrm{valve}} + \sum_{\mathrm{chambers}} p\,\frac{V}{\beta}\dot{p}\\ &\quad + \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 IQ_h^2 + \tfrac12 m\dot{y}^2 + mgy\right)\\ &\quad + \Delta p_fQ_h + F_f\dot{y} + F_w\dot{y} - F_{\mathrm{stop}}\dot{y}. \end{aligned} \tag{19.1}$$

The page integrates every term separately and checks the sum against the pump work at each sample. The residual stays at $10^{-14}$ of the pump work: a test that every force in the mechanics matches its flow partner in the hydraulics.

20 How the computer solves it

Both pages use the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method. From $\mathbf{x}_n$ it samples the slope four times across a step $h$ and averages them with weights 1, 2, 2, 1:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{k}_1 &= \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_n),\\ \mathbf{k}_2 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{k}_1\right),\\ \mathbf{k}_3 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{k}_2\right),\\ \mathbf{k}_4 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_n + h\,\mathbf{k}_3\right),\\ \mathbf{x}_{n+1} &= \mathbf{x}_n + \tfrac{h}{6}\left(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4\right). \end{aligned} \tag{20.1}$$

The local error is of order $h^5$ and the global error of order $h^4$: halving the step cuts the error sixteen-fold. To see what that means, apply one step to the test equation $\dot{x} = \lambda x$, whose exact solution grows by $e^{\lambda h}$ per step. Writing $z = \lambda h$: $\mathbf{k}_1 = \lambda x$, $\mathbf{k}_2 = \lambda x(1 + z/2)$, $\mathbf{k}_3 = \lambda x(1 + z/2 + z^2/4)$, $\mathbf{k}_4 = \lambda x(1 + z + z^2/2 + z^3/4)$. The weighted average multiplies $x$ by

$$R(\lambda h) = 1 + \lambda h + \frac{(\lambda h)^2}{2} + \frac{(\lambda h)^3}{6} + \frac{(\lambda h)^4}{24}.$$

These are the first five terms of $e^{\lambda h}$, so the error per step starts at $(\lambda h)^5/120$: the order $h^5$ quoted above. The method is stable while $|R| \le 1$, which on the negative real axis means $\lambda h \ge -2.79$.

In our press
The fastest mode of the servo model is the small valve-port volume charged through a wide-open valve while the pressure drop across it is small, where the rounded orifice law (8.3) is steepest: $\lambda \approx \beta K_v/(V_a\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{tr}}}) \approx 1.6\times10^4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. With $h = 10^{-5}$ s, $\lambda h \approx 0.16$, far inside the stable region. Stops are located inside a step by bisection on the step length, to $10^{-12}$ s.
